Opening Remarks

The Parlor

Imagine that you enter a parlor. You come late. When you arrive, others have long preceded you, and they are engaged in a heated discussion, a discussion too heated for them to pause and tell you exactly what it is about. Burke (1941)

Huff (1999) uses Burke’s parlor to describe scholarly publishing: a paper enters a discussion that started long before it and will continue after it. Reviewers and editors judge a manuscript by whether it understands that discussion and adds something the participants care about. Positioning is the work of identifying which discussion your paper joins and showing that you have listened before you speak.

Learning Outcomes

After completing this unit, you will be able to:

  1. Locate your manuscript within a discipline, a literature strand, and a scholarly conversation.
  2. Map a conversation by its core outlets, authors, and concepts.
  3. Evaluate target outlets based on rankings, genres, and fit with your conversation.
  4. Distinguish reputable outlets from predatory and questionable publishers.

Conversations

Scholarship as Conversation

Scientific work can be viewed as a conversation among scholars (Huff, 1999).

  • Conversations take place in journals and at conferences.
  • They determine which questions count as interesting and which answers count as convincing.
  • Only some ideas lead to further discussion; many are ignored.
  • No conversation covers everything that could be said about a project.

Treating scholarship as conversation has practical consequences for writing. The relevant literature for your paper is the set of texts your conversation partners have written and read, which is narrower than “everything published on the topic.” Your research gap only counts as a gap if the participants of that conversation recognize it as one, and your contribution has to be phrased in the terms they use.

The conversation also shapes what you study. Successful scholars listen first: they read what the community discusses, which arguments it has dismissed, and which open questions it considers worth pursuing (Huff, 1999).

Levels of Positioning

The four levels narrow down the audience step by step. The discipline sets the general standards of rigor and the accepted methods. The association and its subgroups (divisions at the Academy of Management, special interest groups at the Association for Information Systems, scientific commissions in the German VHB) organize the people who review your work and meet at conferences. The literature strand is the broader body of work that serves as your reference point. The conversation is the specific, ongoing debate within that strand: a limited set of papers that cite each other, use shared concepts, and address related questions.

Doctoral candidates often stop at the level of the literature strand (“I contribute to the digital innovation literature”). At that level, the claim is too broad for a reviewer to evaluate. Naming the conversation makes the positioning testable: a reader can check whether you know its key papers, use its concepts correctly, and add something to it.

Recognizing a Conversation

  • Shared questions: papers address related problems and cite each other.
  • Shared concepts: a stable vocabulary of concepts and constructs.
  • Shared venues: special issues, conference tracks, and recurring publication clusters.
  • Core authors: a limited group of scholars who contribute and review.
  • Dominant procedures: typical theories and methods that set the quality standard.

Conversations become visible in several places (Huff, 1999). Special issue calls and conference track descriptions summarize what a community currently considers important. Recent review articles reconstruct the state of a conversation and name its open questions. Citation patterns show which papers serve as shared reference points: if you find that the same ten papers appear in the reference lists of most recent contributions, you have found the core of a conversation.

The active researchers in a conversation also review for its outlets. They set the quality standards, decide which theoretical and methodological approaches are accepted, and determine which outlets count as good places to publish. Knowing who these people are helps you anticipate reviewer expectations.

Joining a Conversation

How authors construct the conversation they join (Locke & Golden-Biddle, 1997):

Locke & Golden-Biddle (1997) analyzed how published papers in organization studies construct their contribution. Their central observation is that authors do not find a conversation ready-made; they actively construct it by selecting and arranging prior work. The construction determines what kind of contribution becomes possible.

  • Synthesized coherence links studies that were not previously seen as related. The contribution consists of revealing a common question that the separate works share.
  • Progressive coherence presents a cumulative line of research with shared concepts and methods. The contribution extends the line one step further.
  • Non-coherence presents a field in disagreement. The contribution helps to resolve, reframe, or explain the disagreement.

The choice is strategic: the same body of literature can be presented in different ways, and each presentation prepares the reader for a different contribution. The second step of their framework, problematizing the constructed literature, is covered in the Contribution unit.

Outlets

Outlet Choice

Your work is not suitable for every journal, and not every journal is suitable for your work.

  • Conversation fit: Does the outlet publish the conversation you join?
  • Genre fit: Does it publish your type of paper?
  • Level fit: Does the ranking match the maturity of your contribution?
  • Practical fit: turnaround time, open access fees, and the requirements of your doctoral regulations

Outlet choice follows from positioning: the outlets that publish your conversation are the candidates, because their reviewers and readers are the people your paper addresses. Within that set, the paper type and the maturity of the contribution narrow the choice further.

Decide on target outlets together with your supervisors and co-authors. A high-ranking target only makes sense if everyone agrees that the contribution and the data can reach that level, because a submission to such a journal often takes one to two years and several revision rounds. Check the regulations of your doctoral program as well: cumulative dissertations often require a minimum number of papers in outlets of a certain ranking category.

Rankings

Pro tip: The browser extension Rapid Journal Quality Check shows journal rankings (e.g., VHB, ABS, FT50, SJR, CORE) directly next to Google Scholar search results.

The VHB-Rating 2024 replaces JOURQUAL 3 as the reference ranking of the German Academic Association for Business Research (Verband der Hochschullehrerinnen und Hochschullehrer für Betriebswirtschaft, 2024); many German business schools and doctoral programs use it to evaluate publications. Other disciplines use their own lists: economics, psychology, and computer science each have established rankings, and for computer science conferences, the CORE ranking plays a major role.

Rankings evaluate outlets, not papers, and they differ in method: some rely on expert surveys, others on citation metrics. They also lag behind developments in a field. Use them to understand the expectations of your community and the requirements of your program, and use the conversation map to decide where your paper belongs. In interdisciplinary programs such as DIWAG, check which ranking your discipline and your supervisors refer to.

The free extension Rapid Journal Quality Check (Chrome and Edge, with a port for Firefox) speeds up this check while you search the literature: it displays the ranking of a journal or conference next to each Google Scholar result and on author profiles, and you select which lists it shows. Verify important decisions against the official ranking, because the lists in the extension may lag behind the latest editions, and review the permissions the extension requests before you install it.

Paper Genres

Journals publish different types of papers, each with its own structure and review criteria.

  • Empirical: quantitative, qualitative, or mixed-methods studies
  • Conceptual: theory development without new data
  • Review: systematic, narrative, or theory-building reviews of a literature
  • Design-oriented: artifacts and design knowledge (e.g., design science research)
  • Short formats: research notes, commentaries, research-in-progress papers

Journals differ in which genres they publish and how they expect each genre to look. Many journals have separate categories (e.g., “research article,” “research note,” “review,” “issues and opinions”) with different length limits and review criteria. Check whether your target journal offers a category for your type of paper, read the description, and download recent examples of that category to learn its structure.

Thatcher (2025) summarizes the tendencies of four Information Systems journals from his experience: MIS Quarterly favors mid-range, construct-centered theory with strong empirical tests; Information Systems Research favors mechanism-rich theorizing and methodological rigor, often in multi-study designs; Journal of Management Information Systems is open to design-oriented work, modeling, and simulations with a strong emphasis on problem relevance; and the Journal of the Association for Information Systems accepts conceptual and essayistic papers. Treat such characterizations as starting hypotheses to verify with recent issues.

Model the Journal

Reverse-engineer three to five recent exemplars from your target journal (Thatcher, 2025).

  • How do introductions open and where does the research question appear?
  • How are theory and hypotheses or propositions connected?
  • How are methods and results presented (tables, figures, robustness)?
  • How do discussions close?

Reviewers expect a submission to read like it belongs in their journal, in topic, structure, and voice. Thatcher (2025) proposes a test: if someone removed the title page of your manuscript and placed it in the middle of a published issue, it should not feel out of place.

Select exemplars that are similar to your paper in topic and genre, and map their structure: the order and length of sections, the paragraph flow of the introduction, the transition from theory to hypotheses, the format of tables, and the closing of the discussion. Adapt the structure to your paper rather than copying it.

Conferences

In many fields, conferences are a regular step on the way to a journal publication.

  • Conferences provide feedback and visibility before journal submission.
  • Conference tracks show current conversations and their participants.
  • Doctoral consortia offer feedback from senior scholars.
  • Check the prior-publication policy of your target journal.

In many fields, including Information Systems (e.g., ICIS, ECIS, HICSS, and the German Wirtschaftsinformatik conference), conferences are part of the publication path: a research-in-progress or full conference paper gathers feedback, and an extended version later goes to a journal. Journals differ in how much overlap they accept between a conference paper and a journal submission, so check the policy before you publish the conference version. In computer science, by contrast, top conferences are primary outlets in their own right.

Predatory Journals

Predatory journals prioritize self-interest at the expense of scholarship (Grudniewicz et al., 2019).

  • False or misleading information
  • Deviation from best editorial and publication practices
  • Lack of transparency
  • Aggressive and indiscriminate solicitation

Grudniewicz et al. (2019) developed a consensus definition in a Delphi study with researchers, publishers, and librarians: “Predatory journals and publishers are entities that prioritize self-interest at the expense of scholarship and are characterized by false or misleading information, deviation from best editorial and publication practices, a lack of transparency, and/or the use of aggressive and indiscriminate solicitation practices.”

Publishing in such outlets damages your reputation, wastes your data (a paper published there can rarely be republished elsewhere), and may count against you in evaluations.

Documented Cases

Several investigations and legal cases have exposed predatory and questionable practices.

  • Sting operation: a deliberately flawed fake paper was accepted by 157 of 304 open-access journals (Bohannon, 2013).
  • Fake editor: a fictitious scholar was accepted as editor by 48 of 360 journals she applied to; none of them was listed in the Journal Citation Reports (Sorokowski et al., 2017).
  • Court ruling: in 2019, a US federal court ordered the publisher OMICS International to pay more than USD 50 million for deceptive publishing practices.
  • Mass retractions: in 2023, Wiley retracted thousands of papers from special issues of its Hindawi journals after detecting paper-mill activity (Van Noorden, 2023).

Bohannon (2013) submitted versions of a fabricated paper on an anticancer agent, written by a fictitious author from a fictitious institute, to 304 open-access journals. The paper contained errors that any competent reviewer should have detected; more than half of the journals accepted it, many without any substantive review.

Sorokowski et al. (2017) created the profile of a fictitious researcher, “Anna O. Szust” (Polish oszust means “fraud”), with no publications and a fake CV, and applied on her behalf for editorial positions at 360 journals drawn from Beall’s list, the Directory of Open Access Journals, and the Journal Citation Reports. Forty-eight journals accepted her, almost all of them from Beall’s list; none of the journals listed in the Journal Citation Reports did.

The case against OMICS International was brought by the US Federal Trade Commission, which accused the publisher of misrepresenting its peer review, its editorial boards, and its fees. The ruling shows that predatory publishing can be a legal matter, not only a reputational one.

The Hindawi retractions illustrate the less obvious categories in the typology that follows: the affected journals were indexed and published by an established publisher, but parts of their special-issue programs had been infiltrated by paper mills (Van Noorden, 2023).

Questionable Outlets

The boundary between predatory and legitimate publishing is not sharp. Next to classic predatory journals, which are easy to spot, there are publishers that are indexed in major databases but run large numbers of special issues with compromised review, journals associated with paper mills, and commercially driven publishers whose practices vary between journals.

Practical checks before submission:

  • Do you and your supervisors know the journal, and do papers you read cite it?
  • Is it listed in the rankings your community uses?
  • Are the editorial board members real, active researchers in the field, and do they list the journal on their own websites?
  • Are the review process, the fees, and the timelines transparent?
  • Did you receive an unsolicited invitation with flattering language and a short deadline?

The Think. Check. Submit. initiative provides a checklist for these questions. Lists of suspected predatory journals (e.g., Beall’s list, which is now maintained anonymously) can serve as a warning signal but are neither complete nor authoritative.
