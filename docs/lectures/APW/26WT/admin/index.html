Introduction

Motivation

Write so scholars will cite it, but others will understand it. Thatcher (2025)

Thatcher calls this standard the “May Maxim,” after his mentor, the historian Dean May. It sets the bar for this course: a paper has to meet the rigor expectations of a specific scholarly community and still be clear enough that a smart reader from outside that community understands what it claims and why it matters.

Contents

The course takes a manuscript you are working on and moves it closer to submission: you position it in a scholarly conversation, sharpen its argument, check its theoretical and methodological foundations, and prepare it for the review process.

The core curriculum covers:

  • Positioning and outlets: discipline, literature strand, and conversation; journal rankings, genres, and questionable outlets
  • Research gaps and contributions: gap spotting, problematization, and contribution statements (Corley & Gioia, 2011; Sandberg & Alvesson, 2011)
  • Manuscript architecture: paper structure by genre, introductions and abstracts, paragraph logic, concept-centric argumentation (Thatcher, 2025; Webster & Watson, 2002)
  • Argument and revision: mechanisms, discussion and boundary conditions, style, and systematic revision diagnostics
  • Theorizing and paradigms: deductive, inductive, and abductive pathways; the alignment of paradigm, theory, method, and claims (Bendoly & Oliva, 2025; Orlikowski & Baroudi, 1991)
  • Review and publication: submission, editorial decisions, response letters, and scholarly communities

Expectations

The course expects five contributions from each participant.

  • Regular attendance on all three days
  • Active participation in discussions and peer rounds
  • Provision of your own work for discussion
  • Written reviews and a decision letter for the manuscripts of your peers
  • Preparation of the materials provided

Learning Outcomes

After completing this course, you will be able to:

Positioning

Position your contribution precisely within a discipline, a literature strand, and a scholarly conversation.

Evaluate target outlets based on relevant rankings and genres, and distinguish reputable outlets from predatory journals.

Writing

Structure single manuscripts (abstract, introduction, main part, discussion) for their audience, with clear paragraph logic and concept-centric argumentation.

Articulate research gaps precisely and align the line of argument consistently with the state of research.

Knowledge Process

Reflect critically on your research path with regard to deductive, inductive, and abductive logics of theory development, and justify it methodologically.

Analyze the relationship between the central concepts and constructs of your conversation, the methods applied, and the underlying paradigms (e.g., positivism, interpretivism).

Publishing

Provide qualified peer feedback on your own and others’ drafts, and process review comments constructively.

Manage submissions, editorial decisions, and interactions with journals and scholarly communities (e.g., divisions, special interest groups, doctoral consortia) independently.

Learning Set-Up

Design

The course runs on three on-site days at HNU and works with the manuscripts you bring.

  • Each block combines a short input with an exercise on your own manuscript.
  • In peer rounds, the group works on each manuscript in turn (e.g., as editors in a desk-reject simulation).
  • Between the second and the third day, you revise your manuscript and act as reviewer and editor for your peers in a mock review round.

Effort

You will gain 1 ECTS for this course.

  • Joint learning time (three on-site days): approx. 40%
  • Self-study (preparation, revision, written peer review): approx. 60%

There is no graded assessment. The course builds on the course Paper Reviewing, Writing, and Publishing and is intended for doctoral candidates from their second semester onward.

NoteConfidentiality and AI Use

The manuscripts of your peers are unpublished work and confidential. Do not upload them, in full or in part, to AI tools (e.g., ChatGPT, Claude, Copilot, DeepL). Read the manuscripts yourself and write your feedback, including the reviews over the holidays, without AI support.

Before Days 1 and 2

Please upload your manuscript package by Monday, 07.12.2026:

  1. Manuscript: a full draft of one paper you are working on, including at least a draft of the discussion section. Name the file with your full name first (e.g., Full-Name_Manuscript.pdf).
  2. Conversation map (one page): details see below.
  3. Positioning sheet (one page): discipline, association and subgroup (e.g., division, special interest group), literature strand, conversation, target outlet, and one sentence stating your intended contribution; use the template.
  4. Aims and scope (up to two pages): profile, aims and scope, and fit of your target journal; use the template. A peer uses it as editor in the desk-reject simulation, and the two or three papers you name under fit serve as your exemplar papers on the second day.

Before the first day, read Thatcher (2025) (Rules 1 to 17 and Appendix E) and the conversation maps, positioning sheets, and aims and scope of all peers in the Moodle folder Manuscript Package. You do not need to read the manuscripts of your peers in advance; you work with them in the exercises. Also read the introductions of the exemplar papers named in your aims and scope and note where they place the motivation, the research question, and the contribution (about one hour).

In the evening of the first day, revise your contribution sentence based on the desk-reject simulation and the assumption hunt. If you changed your target outlet in the outlet shortlist, read the introduction of one exemplar from the new outlet.

Conversation Map

Before you write your positioning sheet, reconstruct the conversation your manuscript joins from the literature itself and create your conversation map. Plan about two to three hours.

  1. Seed: select two to three papers from your reference list that you consider central to your manuscript.
  2. Citation chasing: trace the conversation backward (the references of the seed papers) and forward (“Cited by” in Google Scholar), focusing on recent papers.
  3. Core identification: identify the papers that cite each other repeatedly; they form the core of the conversation. Note the most recent review article or special issue.
  4. Conversation map: name the conversation in one phrase and list a maximum of five journals, five authors, and five concepts or constructs; use the template.
  5. Self-check: mark which of the core papers your manuscript cites.

Upload your conversation map as part of your manuscript package by Monday, 07.12.2026. You present your map on the first day in about five minutes.

Tools such as Connected Papers, ResearchRabbit, or Litmaps generate citation graphs from seed papers and show clusters of papers that cite each other. The browser extension Rapid Journal Quality Check displays journal rankings next to Google Scholar results, which helps to identify the leading outlets of the conversation.

Before Day 3

The self-study between the second and the third day simulates a review round.

  • By 28.12.2026: upload your revised manuscript.
  • By 07.01.2027, 12:00: write two reviews as reviewer for the manuscripts of two peers, addressed to their target journals; use the template. Do not open the other review of a manuscript you review before you have uploaded your own.
  • 07.01.2027, afternoon: read the two reviews of the manuscript you edit.
  • Third day: write the decision letter as editor, using the template, then draft a response matrix to the reviews and the decision letter you received.

Each manuscript receives two reviews and one decision letter, and each participant takes every role once: author, reviewer (twice), and editor. A review contains a short summary of the manuscript, the main concerns (positioning, contribution, theory, method, presentation), minor points, and a recommendation (reject, major revision, minor revision, accept). Number your points so that the author can respond to each one. Comment on argument, positioning, and structure first; language comes last.

As editor, you read the two reviews of your manuscript on the afternoon of 07.01.2027 and write the decision letter on the third day. The decision letter states the decision, synthesizes the reviews into the three to five issues the revision has to address, and indicates where the reviewers disagree and which position the editor supports. The lecturer acts as editor-in-chief and comments on the decision letters in the debrief.

Methods and Paradigms

Complete three short tasks on reasoning, paradigms, and methods.

  1. Look up deductive, inductive, and abductive reasoning and classify your manuscript.
  2. Note keywords on positivist, interpretive, and critical research.
  3. Add the dominant methods of your conversation to your conversation map.
