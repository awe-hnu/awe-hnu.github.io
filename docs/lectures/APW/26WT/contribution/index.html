Opening Remarks

Interesting Research

Readers find a theory interesting when it denies an assumption they hold (Davis, 1971).

Davis (1971) analyzed which sociological theories became influential and concluded that their truth alone did not explain their impact. Influential theories challenged an assumption their audience took for granted: what seemed to be stable turned out to be unstable, what seemed to be individual turned out to be collective. Theories that confirmed what the audience already believed were judged obvious; theories that denied assumptions the audience did not hold were judged irrelevant.

The observation shifts the question from “Where is the gap?” to “What does my audience assume, and what does my study show about it?”

Learning Outcomes

After completing this unit, you will be able to:

  1. Classify research gaps by type and by the way they are constructed.
  2. Apply problematization to identify assumptions in your conversation.
  3. Locate your contribution along the dimensions of originality and utility.
  4. Formulate a one-sentence contribution statement for your manuscript.
  5. Assess the positioning of your peers’ manuscripts.

Research Gaps

Gap Types

Research gaps differ in the kind of knowledge that is missing.

Gap typologies help you name what kind of knowledge your study adds. The first three types (theoretical, empirical, methodological) are the most common in the literature on research questions (Thatcher, 2025a); conceptual and contextual gaps often appear as subtypes or as arguments for a study rather than as contributions in their own right.

Contextual gaps deserve particular caution. “Nobody has studied X in Germany” only becomes a contribution when the context changes the explanation: when you can argue why the established theory should work differently in the new setting and what that difference teaches us about the theory.

Gap Spotting

Three modes of constructing research questions from gaps (Sandberg & Alvesson, 2011):

Sandberg & Alvesson (2011) analyzed 52 articles in leading management journals and found that gap spotting is the dominant way researchers construct research questions. Neglect spotting, in particular, appears in most papers. The modes share a logic: the existing literature is accepted as basically sound, and the study adds to it by filling a hole, resolving a confusion, or extending its reach.

Gap-Spotting Limits

Gap spotting, the dominant way of constructing research questions, has four limits (Sandberg & Alvesson, 2011).

  • That a topic is unstudied does not make it worth studying.
  • Gap spotting accepts the assumptions of existing theory.
  • It tends to produce incremental contributions.
  • It is the safer and less impactful route (Thatcher, 2025a).

Reviewers at leading journals regularly reject papers whose motivation rests on a gap alone (“no study has examined X”). The question they ask is: why should anyone care that X has not been studied? A convincing argument shows what the missing knowledge prevents us from understanding or doing.

Sandberg & Alvesson (2011) argue that the dominance of gap spotting reduces the chance of interesting theory, because it reproduces the assumptions of the literature it builds on. Gap spotting remains legitimate, and for many doctoral papers it is the realistic choice. Its limits matter when you write the introduction: the gap has to be connected to a problem the conversation recognizes.

Problematization

Definition

Problematization identifies and challenges the assumptions underlying existing theory (Alvesson & Sandberg, 2011).

Alvesson & Sandberg (2011) propose problematization as an alternative to gap spotting. Instead of adding to existing theory, the researcher questions the assumptions on which it rests. The five assumption types range from narrow (in-house assumptions within one school) to broad (field assumptions shared by competing schools). Challenging in-house assumptions is the most feasible; challenging paradigm or field assumptions has more impact but is harder to make acceptable to reviewers.

The authors outline a methodology in six steps:

  1. Identify a domain of literature.
  2. Identify and articulate the assumptions underlying it.
  3. Evaluate the articulated assumptions.
  4. Develop an alternative assumption ground.
  5. Consider the alternative in relation to its audience.
  6. Evaluate the alternative assumption ground.

Step 5 links back to Davis (1971): an alternative is only interesting if it challenges an assumption the audience actually holds, and only acceptable if it does not challenge so many assumptions that the audience rejects it outright.

Problematizing Modes

How published papers problematize the literature they construct (Locke & Golden-Biddle, 1997):

  • Incomplete: the literature is not finished; the study fills in.
  • Inadequate: the literature overlooks a perspective that changes the picture.
  • Incommensurate: the literature is misguided; the study offers an alternative.

Locke & Golden-Biddle (1997) describe problematization as the second move after constructing the conversation. The three modes differ in how strongly they challenge prior work. An incomplete framing preserves the literature and adds to it (close to gap spotting). An inadequate framing argues that an overlooked perspective would change the understanding of the phenomenon. An incommensurate framing argues that the literature is wrong and proposes a different account.

The stronger the challenge, the stronger the evidence and argument it requires, and the more likely reviewers from the challenged camp are to resist. Choose the mode that your data and argument can carry.

Phenomenon-Driven Inquiry

Start from an emerging phenomenon and identify the theoretical puzzle behind it (Thatcher, 2025a).

  • The phenomenon provides relevance (e.g., generative AI in knowledge work).
  • The puzzle provides the contribution: what existing theory fails to explain about it.
  • The route is harder and potentially more impactful than gap spotting.

Phenomenon-driven research begins with a development in practice that existing theory struggles to explain. Its risk lies in staying descriptive: documenting a new phenomenon is not yet a theoretical contribution. The contribution emerges when the study shows what the phenomenon reveals about an existing theory, for example a boundary condition, a missing mechanism, or an assumption that no longer holds. Thatcher (2025a) recommends that doctoral candidates weigh the route against their risk tolerance, their work context, and their motivation.

Contribution

Contribution Dimensions

Corley & Gioia (2011) describe theoretical contributions along two dimensions.

Corley & Gioia (2011) reviewed the criteria reviewers and editors use to judge theoretical contributions and condensed them into two dimensions. Originality ranges from incremental insight (advancing understanding step by step) to revelatory insight (revealing what was not seen before). Utility ranges from scientific usefulness (improving conceptual rigor or the potential for testing) to practical usefulness (applicability to problems that managers and organizations face).

Leading journals expect revelatory insight, but most doctoral papers make incremental contributions, which is legitimate when the increment is clearly stated. Overclaiming a revelatory contribution undermines credibility; underclaiming hides the value of the study.

Contribution Statement

Lead with what you show, not with what the literature lacks (Thatcher, 2025b).

We contribute by [mechanism or boundary condition] explaining [X → Y] for [literature or audience].

  • Weak: “With the growing use of information systems, researchers have become increasingly interested in user attitudes.”
  • Strong: “We define IT mindfulness as a user’s dynamic capability and validate its influence on task adaptation and feature use.”

Thatcher (2025b) recommends that the abstract and the introduction state the contribution within the first sentences. The weak example from his handbook delays the contribution with generic background; the strong example names the construct, the claim, and the effect in a single sentence.

The template is a diagnostic, not a sentence for the published paper. If you cannot complete it, the contribution is not yet clear to you. A second test is the plain-language version: “This study shows that [core insight] and explains why it matters for [audience or context].”

The recommendation to lead with the contribution appears to conflict with the classic introduction formula, which moves from motivation through the state of research and the gap to the research question. Both are compatible: the gap motivates the study, and the contribution states its result. The architecture of the introduction is the subject of the Manuscript Architecture unit.

Homework

Before the second day:

  1. Finalize your contribution sentence based on the desk-reject simulation and the assumption hunt.
  2. If you changed your target outlet, read the introduction of one exemplar paper from the new outlet.
