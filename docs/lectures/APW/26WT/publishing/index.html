Opening Remarks

Make It Easy

Your job is to make it easy to say yes. Thatcher (2025b)

Thatcher (2025b) (Rule 15) observes that most papers are not rejected because they are wrong, but because they are hard to verify, hard to follow, or hard to trust. Editors and reviewers work under time pressure; a manuscript that anticipates their questions and makes verification effortless turns them into advocates.

This unit follows a manuscript through the publication process: submission, the editor’s decision, the response to reviewers, and the communities in which the work circulates. It starts with the mock review the participants completed during the self-study phase.

Learning Outcomes

After completing this unit, you will be able to:

  1. Prepare a complete submission, including authorship agreements and a cover letter.
  2. Interpret editorial decisions and choose an adequate response.
  3. Respond to reviewers with a structured response matrix and letter.
  4. Use scholarly communities and doctoral consortia for feedback and visibility.
  5. Outline a research agenda that connects your papers.

Mock Review Debrief

Submission

Authorship

Four agreements prevent authorship conflicts in collaborative research.

  • Agree on who is an author and the order before writing the final draft.
  • All authors carry full responsibility for the content.
  • Ask about the policies of your supervisor and your research group.
  • Document contributions, e.g., with the CRediT roles (Brand et al., 2015).

Authorship conflicts are among the most common problems in collaborative research, and they are easiest to avoid when the agreement comes early. The author team can be extended at a later stage, but before submission, and only if all authors agree and the order is aligned. Many journals now ask for a contributor statement; the CRediT taxonomy describes 14 roles (e.g., conceptualization, methodology, formal analysis, writing of the original draft, review and editing) that make individual contributions transparent (Brand et al., 2015).

For cumulative dissertations, check the regulations of your doctoral program: they often require a statement on your own contribution to each co-authored paper and may limit the number of co-authors or the required author position.

Submission Checklist

Check seven points before you submit a manuscript.

  • Text, tables, and figures are numbered consecutively; each table and figure is referenced in the text.
  • Every table and figure is understandable without the text.
  • Every in-text citation appears in the reference list, and vice versa; every reference is accurate.
  • The manuscript meets the requirements of the journal (length, format, anonymization, abstract).
  • Statements are ready: data availability, ethics approval, conflicts of interest, use of AI tools.
  • Author data for all co-authors are at hand (affiliations, contact details, biographies).
  • The final version has been read twice, the second time on a different day.

The checklist combines the formal checks from the previous course with the transparency templates in Thatcher (2025b, Appendix H), which address sensitive data and non-standard design choices. Thatcher reports rejecting a paper because several sources were miscited: if the citations cannot be trusted, reviewers stop trusting the empirics as well (Thatcher, 2025b, Rule 12).

After submission, save the confirmation, inform your co-authors, and send them all submitted files, including the editable versions (e.g., Word and Excel files) if you submitted PDFs.

Cover Letter

A cover letter tells the editor in a few sentences why the manuscript fits the journal.

Dear Professor [Name],

Please find enclosed our manuscript “[Title],” which we submit for consideration as a research article in [Journal].

The manuscript examines [research question] in [context]. Drawing on [theory] and [data and method], we show that [core finding]. The study contributes to the conversation on [conversation] in [Journal], in particular to recent work by [authors published in the journal], by [contribution].

The manuscript has not been published and is not under consideration elsewhere. All authors have approved the submission. [Required statements, e.g., on related conference papers or the use of AI tools.]

Sincerely, [Corresponding author]

A cover letter does not replace the abstract; it tells the editor in a few sentences why the manuscript belongs in this journal. The strongest argument is the conversation: naming the journal’s own recent contributions to the topic shows the editor that the paper continues a discussion the journal already hosts. Include all statements the journal requires, and disclose related publications such as an earlier conference version. Some journals ask for suggested or excluded reviewers; suggest scholars from the conversation without conflicts of interest.

Decisions

Decision Types

Editors communicate one of six typical decisions.

The labels differ between journals; read the letter for what the editor asks, not only for the label. A major revision at a selective journal is an invitation to continue, not a verdict, and the effort of a careful revision is usually worth it. Withdrawing after a major revision makes sense only if the requested changes would turn the paper into a different paper that the authors do not want to write.

After an acceptance without revision, nothing remains to do until the proofs arrive, apart from a short note to thank the editor. After a minor revision, consider each suggestion and make the changes that improve the paper; explain in the response why you did not follow a recommendation.

Handling Rejection

Four practices help you turn a rejection into the next submission.

  • Take time before reacting: at least one night’s sleep.
  • Use the reviews: address the comments before submitting elsewhere.
  • Expect overlap: the next journal may send the paper to the same reviewer.
  • Contest a decision only if the judgment was clearly superficial or wrong, and only with a brief, polite letter.

Rejection is the most frequent outcome in academic publishing and is part of every career. The reviews of a rejected paper are free expert feedback; ignoring them risks meeting the same reviewer again with the same objections, which happens more often than authors expect in small fields. Appeals rarely succeed and should be reserved for clear errors, such as a review that addresses a different paper or rests on a factual misunderstanding.

Responding

Response Letter

A response letter follows five principles.

  • Thank the editor and the reviewers; keep the tone factual throughout.
  • Respond to every point; quote each comment verbatim.
  • State what you changed and where (page, section, table).
  • Disagree with evidence and offer an alternative fix.
  • Start with the editor’s priorities.

The response letter is the document the editor and the reviewers read first in the next round; it determines whether they read the revised manuscript as an improvement. Quoting each comment verbatim and answering directly beneath it lets reviewers verify the changes without searching. When you disagree, explain why, refer to evidence or literature, and offer a different way to address the underlying concern (e.g., a boundary condition, an additional analysis, a clarifying footnote) (Thatcher, 2025b, Appendix G). Reviewers accept a well-argued disagreement more readily than an evasive change.

Response Matrix

Thatcher (2025b) proposes a one-page matrix that turns a revision into a project plan.

Thatcher (2025b) proposes a one-page matrix that turns a revise-and-resubmit into a project plan. Each row contains one discrete issue, quoted verbatim, the location where the issue is visible, the change made, the rule or principle the change applies, and the result (accepted, or rejected with a brief rationale). Number and cluster the issues (e.g., theory, methods, results) so that the editor can scan them, and keep a version date and the review round in the header. The matrix serves as the internal plan; the response letter is written from it.

Community

Scholarly Communities

Four entry points connect doctoral candidates with scholarly communities.

  • Associations and subgroups: divisions of the Academy of Management, special interest groups of the AIS, scientific commissions of the VHB
  • Doctoral consortia at major conferences (e.g., ICIS, ECIS, Academy of Management)
  • PhD seminars, e.g., the AIS SIG DITE PhD Research Seminar
  • Reviewing for conferences as an entry point into a community

The conversations that the first day mapped are carried by communities. Associations organize them in subgroups (divisions, special interest groups, scientific commissions), which run conference tracks, workshops, awards, and doctoral programs. Joining the subgroup that hosts your conversation gives access to the people who will later review your work.

Doctoral consortia bring a small group of doctoral candidates together with senior scholars for feedback on their dissertation projects; admission is competitive and usually requires a short proposal. PhD seminars such as the AIS SIG DITE PhD Research Seminar on digital innovation, transformation, and entrepreneurship run over several weeks, combine readings with discussions led by leading scholars, and end with a paper development workshop. Reviewing for conferences is the easiest way in: it trains the reviewer’s perspective, and track chairs remember reliable reviewers.

Research Agenda

A research agenda is a long-term, strategic roadmap of interrelated studies that builds a scholarly identity (Thatcher, 2025a).

  • It focuses attention on a problem and defines the audience for your work.
  • It helps to decide whether to take on a new paper or learn a new method.
  • Research is messy: stay problem-centric and accept that some papers lay foundations.

Thatcher (2025a) recommends developing a research agenda through the systematic scaffolding of theoretical ideas, the purposeful development of methodological skills, and the explicit incorporation of practical impact. He also stresses that research is rarely linear: it is messy, non-linear, and sometimes opportunistic. The way to add structure to such a process is to stay focused on one problem and to accept that some papers serve as foundations for others.

Paper Sets

Thatcher (2025a) recommends building a research agenda around a paper set.

  • A coherent series of related papers around a core problem
  • Each paper deepens theoretical, empirical, or practical understanding.
  • Not all papers appear in top journals; foundational papers (review, method tutorial, practice paper) prepare the core contribution.
  • The papers are synergistic and build your identity as a scholar (Thatcher, 2025a).

Thatcher (2025a) illustrates paper sets with his work on IT mindfulness: a tutorial and a measurement paper in other outlets built the conceptual and methodological foundation for a construct and theory paper in MIS Quarterly and a field experiment in the Journal of Management Information Systems. The foundational papers showed technical skill, built trust with the community, and framed the conversation.

For cumulative dissertations, the paper set and the dissertation coincide: the framing paper explains how the individual papers address one core problem and how together they make a contribution that exceeds the sum of the parts.
