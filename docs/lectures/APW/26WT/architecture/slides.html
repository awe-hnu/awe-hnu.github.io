Opening Remarks

Claim and Support

Strong papers begin with a claim and end by showing support for it. Thatcher (2025)

The first day dealt with where a manuscript belongs and what it contributes. This unit deals with how the manuscript carries that contribution to the reader: the overall structure, the introduction and abstract, the paragraphs, and the use of concepts. Reviewers form their judgment early, often after the first page, and they read the rest of the paper through that first impression.

Many of the rules in this unit come from Thatcher (2025), who writes about construct-centered, quantitative papers in Information Systems. Most rules transfer to qualitative and conceptual work: a qualitative paper also needs a claim in its first sentences, clear concepts, and consistent labels. Where a rule depends on the type of research, the notes say so.

Learning Outcomes

After completing this unit, you will be able to:

  1. Structure a manuscript according to its genre and the conventions of the target outlet.
  2. Write an introduction that answers the questions reviewers ask first.
  3. Open the introduction and the abstract with a claim instead of background.
  4. Build paragraphs around topic sentences and connect them through their information flow.
  5. Argue concept-centric, with stable definitions and labels across the manuscript.

Paper Structure

Structure by Genre

Each paper genre follows a typical macro-structure.

The structure of a paper depends on its genre and on the journal. The table shows typical macro-structures; the exemplar papers from your target outlet show the variant your reviewers expect. Recker (2021) advises following the standard structure as closely as possible, because readers navigate papers by their expectations, and innovative structures make reading harder.

Qualitative papers often replace “results” with “findings” and present a data structure or an emerging model; design-oriented papers add sections on the artifact and its evaluation. Whatever the genre, the introduction and the discussion carry the argument, and the sections between them provide the evidence.

The Hourglass

Bem (1987) describes the empirical article as an hourglass.

  • The introduction moves from the general problem to the specific question.
  • The middle sections stay narrow: theory, method, and results for this study.
  • The discussion moves from the specific findings back to the general significance.
  • Introduction and conclusion mirror each other (Bem, 1987).

Bem (1987) describes the empirical article as an hourglass: it begins broadly, narrows to the specific study, and widens again in the discussion. The image has a practical consequence: the opening and the closing of a paper address the same question at the same level of generality. If the introduction promises a theoretical insight and the conclusion emphasizes practical implications, the reader perceives a break, which Thatcher (2025) calls “contribution drift.”

Introduction

Three Questions

An effective introduction answers three questions (Grant & Pollock, 2011):

Grant & Pollock (2011) analyzed introductions of award-winning papers in the Academy of Management Journal and condensed their function into three questions. The questions map onto the classic elements of an introduction: motivate the topic, briefly report the state of research, identify the gap or problem, formulate an explicit research question, describe the approach, state the expected contributions to theory and practice, and, depending on the journal, outline the scope and the structure of the paper.

Introductions and conclusions are the hardest parts of a paper because they balance context and focus. Many authors write them last; if you write the introduction first to orient your own thinking, expect to rewrite it once the rest of the paper is done.

Opening Moves

Open with a claim or a puzzle, not with background (Thatcher, 2025).

  • Weak: “The IS field has long been interested in digital transformation.”
  • Strong: “This paper shows that CIO presence, often ignored in emerging markets, substantially predicts a firm’s AI orientation.”

Thatcher (2025) (Rule 1) argues that the first sentence should make a theoretically meaningful claim or present an empirical puzzle. Historical overviews (“Since the 1990s …”), general interest statements (“X is increasingly important …”), and literature openings (“Researchers have long studied …”) delay the value of the paper. Thatcher calls them “throat-clearing.”

The skim test: a busy colleague who reads only the first page can explain what the paper is about, what it finds, and why it matters for theory.

Narrative Hooks

A hook creates tension that the paper resolves (Thatcher, 2025):

  • Paradox: “Why do firms invest in AI but fail to use it strategically?”
  • Surprising fact: “Despite similar resources, some hospitals drastically outperform others in cybersecurity compliance.”
  • Theoretical contradiction: “Current theories predict X, but we observe Y.”

Rule 2 of Thatcher (2025) adds tension to the opening: the reader should want to know what explains the puzzle before learning about the theory or the method. A hook works when the paper’s contribution resolves it; a dramatic opening that the findings do not address raises expectations the paper cannot meet. Three to four lines are usually enough to hook an editor or a reviewer.

Hooks also differ by genre and journal. Some outlets favor openings from practice (a case, a statistic), others favor theoretical puzzles. The exemplar papers show which kind of opening your target journal publishes.

Contribution First

Thatcher (2025) recommends stating the contribution early in the introduction.

  • The gap motivates the study; the contribution states its result.
  • Name the contribution within the first two to three paragraphs.
  • Pass the inverted pyramid test: the most important point comes first.

Thatcher’s recommendation to lead with the contribution (Thatcher, 2025, Rule 3) seems to contradict the classic formula that moves from motivation through the state of research to the gap and the research question. Both fit together: the introduction can open with a claim or puzzle, place the gap in the second or third paragraph, and state the contribution explicitly before the end of the first page. What reviewers object to is an introduction that spends two pages on background before saying what the paper does.

Thatcher (2025, Appendix A.1) proposes a checklist for introductions:

  • Does the first sentence make a claim rather than a background statement?
  • Does the first paragraph introduce a tension that motivates the study?
  • Is the contribution stated within the first two to three paragraphs?
  • Does the opening signal which conversation the paper contributes to?
  • Does the introduction resist turning into a literature review?
  • Can a non-specialist understand why the paper matters after reading only the introduction?

Abstract Elements

An abstract answers four questions about the study.

  1. What the objectives of the study are
  2. How the study was done
  3. Which results it obtained
  4. What the significance of the results is

State the contribution within the first two sentences (Thatcher, 2025).

An abstract is a shortened version of the paper. It has to contain all information a reader needs to decide whether to read the full text; many readers, and many editors at the desk-review stage, read only the abstract. Most authors write it last, once the contribution is settled.

Thatcher (2025) recommends opening with what the study does and finds rather than with what the literature lacks. A strong example from his handbook: “We define IT mindfulness as a user’s dynamic capability and validate its influence on task adaptation and feature use.” Check the word limit and the required format of your target journal: some journals require structured abstracts with fixed headings.

Paragraphs

Topic Sentences

Each paragraph opens with a short claim that the rest of the paragraph develops.

  • The first sentence states the point; the others support it.
  • Reading only the first sentences conveys the argument (Thatcher, 2025).
  • One paragraph, one idea.

Most doctoral candidates write reasonable sentences; the difficulty lies in organizing them into paragraphs. A paragraph begins with a topic sentence that sets the stage for what follows, and every further sentence develops that point. Thatcher (2025) (Rule 3) proposes a diagnostic he calls the topic-sentence ladder: copy the first sentence of each paragraph into a new document. If the sentences read as a coherent argument (claim, reason, implication), the paragraph structure works. If they narrate process (“In this section, we discuss …”) or state background, the paragraphs need a new opening.

Paragraph Logic

Thatcher (2025) describes each paragraph as a sequence of three moves.

  • Setup: the claim or question of the paragraph
  • Action: the argument, evidence, or explanation
  • Implication: what follows for the next step of the argument

Thatcher (2025) (Rule 6) describes paragraphs in theory, methods, and results sections as a chain of setup, action, and implication. Across paragraphs, the logic should follow a chain as well (A leads to B, B leads to C), and parallel ideas should appear in parallel form: if the theory section introduces trust before risk, the hypotheses, the measures, and the discussion keep that order. Changing the order of concepts forces readers to stop and reorient.

Old Before New

Readers expect familiar information at the beginning of a sentence and new information at the end (Gopen & Swan, 1990).

  • The topic position links back to what the reader already knows.
  • The stress position carries the new point.
  • A paragraph can begin with a central term from the last sentence of the preceding paragraph.

Gopen & Swan (1990) analyzed why scientific prose is hard to read even when its sentences are grammatically correct. Their central observation: readers expect a sentence to start with information that connects to what they have just read (topic position) and to end with the information the writer wants to emphasize (stress position). Prose that violates these expectations forces the reader to reconstruct the connections.

The same principle connects paragraphs. Picking up a central term from the end of one paragraph at the beginning of the next creates a smooth transition without explicit connectors such as “furthermore” or “in addition.”

Concepts

Concept-Centric Argumentation

Organize the literature around concepts, not around authors (Webster & Watson, 2002).

  • Author-centric: “Smith (2019) found … Jones (2020) showed … Lee (2021) argued …”
  • Concept-centric: “Digital platforms coordinate complementors through boundary resources (Eaton et al., 2015; Ghazawneh & Henfridsson, 2013) …”

Webster & Watson (2002) distinguish author-centric from concept-centric reviews of the literature. An author-centric text summarizes one study after another and leaves the synthesis to the reader. A concept-centric text organizes the literature around the concepts that matter for the argument and cites several studies in support of each point. The same principle applies to every section that discusses prior work, not only to review papers.

Two practical consequences:

  • Use parenthetical citations unless the author is the point of the sentence (“As Weick (1989) argues …”).
  • Report insights rather than findings: explain why a result occurred and what it means for your argument instead of listing what each study found.

One Construct, One Paragraph

Each focal construct gets its own paragraph with three elements (Thatcher, 2025):

  1. A one-sentence definition
  2. Its boundaries to neighboring constructs
  3. Its position in the nomological network
  • Weak: “IT mindfulness refers to a construct that helps users be better at IT use.”
  • Strong: “IT mindfulness is a user’s dynamic capability to notice new information, remain contextually aware, and adjust IT use intentionally.”

Thatcher (2025) (Rule 4) argues that a precise definition is the foundation of measurement and argument. The weak example is circular and does not say what the construct is or is not; the strong example names what the construct is and how it acts, and it can be distinguished from neighboring constructs such as IT competence (a skill) or IT attention (a narrower cognitive process).

Practical tips from the handbook: write the definition, then state what the construct is not; anchor the definition in a theory; check that the measures reflect the definition. In qualitative and conceptual papers, the same rule applies to the central concepts: define them once, delimit them, and relate them to each other.

Thread the Construct

The example traces how the label of one construct drifts across the sections of a manuscript.

Rule 5 of Thatcher (2025): use the same label and the same framing for a construct from the abstract to the discussion, including tables and figures. Variation that seems stylistically elegant makes reviewers wonder whether the paper describes one construct or several, or whether an old idea is being relabeled. The diagnostic is mechanical: search each construct label with Control-F across the manuscript and standardize every variant (the “Control-F label audit” in Thatcher (2025, Appendix E)).

Label audit

  1. List the three to five central concepts or constructs of your manuscript.
  2. Search each label with Control-F; note every variant and where it appears.
  3. Check whether each concept has a definition paragraph with definition, boundaries, and relations.
