Opening Remarks

Building Explanations

Theorizing turns an observation into a general, testable explanation of it.

The theory unit (U5) was about recognizing a finished theory when you meet one in the literature. This session is about the messier process that produces one: how you get from an observation, something one of your practitioners said, something a pattern in your review keeps surfacing, to an explanation that holds beyond the case that prompted it.

You run that process twice today: once on a classroom observation, and once on an observation of your own.

Learning Outcomes

By the end of this unit, you will be able to:

  • Move a circular or overly specific explanation of an observation to a general, falsifiable one by naming its underlying mechanism.
  • Propose a competing explanation for the same observation, and name a context in which the two would predict different outcomes.
  • Judge a theoretical account against seven quality checks: insight, testability, evidence, parsimony, logic, boundary conditions, and implications.

Theorizing

Definition

Theorizing is the application or development of theoretical arguments to make sense of a real account, for example an observed phenomenon (Recker, 2021).

  • Theorizing can be inductive, deductive, or abductive.
  • Theorizing can be dependent on data analysis, creative thinking, inspiration, or good luck.

Deductive theorizing starts from an existing theory and derives predictions to test against data. Inductive theorizing starts from data and generalizes a pattern out of it. Abductive theorizing starts from a surprising observation and asks which explanation, among several candidates, would best account for it.

The sequence you work through today is abductive: an observation that does not explain itself, a first explanation that fails, a better one, then rival accounts judged against each other. Generating a rival explanation, rather than defending the first account that came to mind, is the step that makes this abduction rather than a guess, and it is the step teams skip most often.

Principles

Theory is always a simplification.

It should not be more complex than the phenomenon it investigates.

A strong theory is an idealization.

A theory simplifies by leaving out whatever does not bear on the relationship it explains. An account of technology adoption that reproduced every factor influencing every user would be as hard to work with as the adoption behaviour itself, so simplification is a condition of the theory being usable at all.

Idealization goes further than leaving detail out: it states the relationship in a form that no observed case matches exactly. Rational decision-making is the standard example. Nobody computes expected utility over a complete option set, and the assumption remains productive because it provides a precise baseline against which observed departures (bounded rationality, heuristics, framing effects) can be measured and explained. An idealization earns its place when deviations from it are informative.

The practical consequence for your own work: once your account starts collecting qualifications so that it fits every case you have seen, it is moving away from a theory and toward a description of your data.

Directions

From Theory to Data

Typically called the “traditional” scientific process.

  • You start with a theory.
  • You develop a modification or an extension of it.
  • Then you collect data specifically to test or falsify the predictions.

From Data to Theory

Often equated with inductive and interpretive research.

  • You start with data, with a study of something that is “really happening.”
  • You examine whether there is a theory to explain what you observe.
  • You start developing a novel theoretical account, “grounded” in the data.

Approaches

Building runs mostly from data to theory, testing mostly from theory to data, and extending moves in both directions.

The approaches are not mutually exclusive, and researchers often combine elements from multiple approaches depending on the research context.

The choice depends on the research question, available data, and the goals of the study.

Directions of inference and kinds of contribution line up only loosely. Building theory usually proceeds from data to theory, testing theory usually proceeds from theory to data, and extending theory does both, taking an established account as its starting point and new observations as the reason to modify it. Treat the pairing as a default, not a rule: theory can also be built deductively out of existing constructs, and tested inductively against cases that should not exist if it held.

Theory Building

Building theory involves the development of new theoretical frameworks or explanatory models to understand a phenomenon. This process often starts with empirical observations or data collection. Researchers use inductive or abductive approaches to identify patterns, relationships, and underlying mechanisms, and formulate conceptual frameworks that capture these patterns. Building theory is particularly useful when there is a lack of established theoretical foundations for a specific area of study (Mueller & Urbach, 2017).

Theory Testing

Testing theory involves subjecting existing theoretical frameworks or models to empirical scrutiny to assess their validity and predictive power. Researchers use deductive testing to generate hypotheses based on established theories and then gather data to confirm or refute these hypotheses. Inductive testing uses empirical observations to challenge or refine existing theories.

Theory Extending

Extending theory refers to enhancing or expanding existing theoretical frameworks to refine and broaden their applicability, accommodating new empirical findings and addressing limitations. Researchers can extend theory by

  • introducing new concepts, dimensions, or variables to an existing theory (conceptual extension);
  • adding new relationships, mechanisms, or components to an existing framework (theoretical extension); or
  • integrating insights and concepts from other disciplines (interdisciplinary extension).

Quality Checks

Recker (2021), Mueller & Urbach (2017), and others propose that you have a good theory when you can answer the following questions:

  • Is your account insightful, challenging, perhaps surprising, and, importantly, does it seem to make sense?
  • Is your account (your arguments) testable (falsifiable)?
  • Do you have convincing evidence to support your account?
  • Is your account parsimonious?
  • Are the arguments logical?
  • What can you say about the boundary conditions of the theory?
  • What are the implications of your theory?

Hold on to the boundary-conditions question in particular. When you reach the synthesis stage of your review and meet studies that appear to contradict one another, apply Bacharach (1989)’s criterion by reconciling them through the contingent factors that explain why one study found X and another found not-X, rather than averaging across them or dismissing the disagreement as noise. That is exactly the move you will need to make in SLR IV: Analysis and Synthesis: treat a contradiction in the literature as a clue to a boundary condition someone has not yet named, rather than a problem to explain away.

You apply three of these seven checks yourself at the end of the team exercise, so keep the list reachable.

Example: Starting Point

Start with an observation.

  • Think about being in college. You’re in class, and the guy next to you, who is obviously a football player, says an unbelievably dumb thing in class. Why?1
  • Initial theory: Football players are dumb.

Theories should be about classes of things, that is, more general.2

  • New theory: Athletes are dumb.

The example comes from Lave & March (1993)’s introduction to speculative model building. It is deliberately trivial, so that the moves stay visible.

Start with the observation itself: a specific person said a specific thing on a specific day. The first explanation most people reach for attaches a trait to the person, or to the group the person belongs to. “Football players are dumb” is consistent with the observation, and it fails as a theory for two separate reasons, scope and circularity, each of which needs its own repair.

The first failure is scope. A claim about football players at one university covers very few cases, so it generates very little that could be tested. Widening the class to athletes costs nothing in explanatory power and buys a much larger set of cases the claim can be checked against. This is the cheapest improvement available at this stage, and it consists purely of restating the same claim about a larger class.

Example: Circularity

Theories should be explanatory, and free of circular arguments, since circularity prevents theories from being falsifiable.

  • Dumbness cannot be directly observed or measured. The only way we can know if people are dumb is by what they say and do. So we say that they say dumb things because they are dumb, a circular argument.
  • New theory: To be a good athlete requires lots of practice time; being smart in class also requires study time. Amount of time is limited, so practicing a sport means less studying, which means being less smart in class.

The second failure is circularity, and widening the class to athletes did nothing about it.

“Dumb” has no measurement independent of the behaviour it is invoked to explain. We infer dumbness from dumb statements, then explain dumb statements by dumbness. Nothing could count as evidence against the claim, which is what makes it unfalsifiable rather than merely wrong.

The repair is to replace the label with a process that could have produced the outcome and that can be observed separately from it. Time is finite, athletic training consumes large amounts of it, and time spent training is unavailable for study. Each link in that chain can be checked on its own, and the account now predicts something specific enough to fail.

Example: Generalizing

A good theory is general enough to generate implications for other groups and contexts, all of which serve as potential tests of the theory, that is, the theory is fertile.

  • The non-circular account names a mechanism and still ties it to sport and coursework. Restating it in terms of any time-consuming activity lets the same reasoning reach other people and situations.
  • New theory: There is limited time in a day, so when a person engages in a very time-consuming activity, such as athletics, it takes away from other very time-consuming activities, such as studying (Limited Time Theory).

Removing the circularity already produced a mechanism, so this step is often misread as introducing one. What changes is scope.

The time-allocation argument says nothing that depends on the activity being a sport, or on the alternative being coursework. Stating it in those general terms makes it fertile: it generates testable implications well beyond the case that prompted it, covering musicians, students working night shifts, and founders taking evening classes.

Each of those populations is a potential test. The general version can be wrong in more ways than the athlete-bound version it replaces, which is what makes it the better theory.

Example: Competing Explanations

Often, there are also other explanations:

  • Everyone has a need to excel in one area. Achieving excellence in any one area is enough to satisfy this need. Football players satisfy their need for accomplishment through football, so they are not motivated to be smart in class (Excellence Theory).
  • We are jealous of others’ success. When we are jealous, we subconsciously lower our evaluation of that person’s performance in other areas. So we think football players ask dumb questions (Jealousy Theory).

We need to study different contexts to see how our theories explain observations.

  • Depending on the context, each theory leads to other expectations, e.g.,
    • Do football players ask dumb questions out of season?
    • Will athletes who do not look like athletes ask dumb questions?
  • The expectations of our theory lead to hypotheses that can be tested by empirical research.

Limited Time is a defensible account of the observation, which does not make it the correct one. Two rival accounts explain the same observation through different mechanisms. Excellence locates the cause in motivation rather than in available hours. Jealousy relocates the cause entirely, out of the football player and into the observer, so that the behaviour being explained becomes our judgement rather than their statement.

Three accounts that all fit the observation are separated by finding contexts in which they disagree. That is the work the two context questions do, and the mapping is worth making explicit.

Out of season. Training time collapses, so Limited Time predicts the dumb questions stop. Excellence predicts they continue, since the need for accomplishment is still satisfied by being a football player. The question separates those two cleanly, and leaves Jealousy untouched, because our evaluation of a football player does not depend on the calendar.

Athletes who do not look like athletes. They still train, so Limited Time and Excellence both predict the behaviour continues. Jealousy predicts it disappears, because the observer has no visible success to discount. The question isolates Jealousy against the other two.

Taken together, the two questions discriminate all three accounts. That is the standard to hold your own competing explanations to in the team exercise.

The Theorizing Loop

  • Observation: something a practitioner said, or a pattern your review keeps surfacing.
  • Generalize: move from a specific instance to a class of things.
  • Mechanism: replace a circular label with the process that actually produces the outcome.
  • Competing explanation: propose at least one alternative account of the same observation.
  • Discriminating test: name the context where the two accounts predict different outcomes.

The five steps condense what the classroom example did at length, and the order is deliberate.

Observation before explanation. Start from something that was actually said, measured, or recorded, stated in the words it arrived in. An observation already phrased as an explanation, “the teams resist change,” has skipped this step, and everything built on it inherits the assumption.

Generalize before testing. A claim about one team in one company covers very few cases, so very little can be checked against it. Widening to a class of cases costs no explanatory power and buys a much larger set of situations the claim has to survive.

Mechanism before rivals. Generalizing a circular claim produces a wider circular claim: “football players are dumb” becomes “athletes are dumb,” no more falsifiable than it started. The circularity goes away once you name a process that could have produced the outcome and that can be observed without observing the outcome itself.

Rival before test. One account that fits the evidence tells you little, since accounts that fit are easy to produce. A useful rival explains the same observation through a genuinely different process, rather than restating a weaker version of the account you already prefer.

Test last. The discriminating context is where the two accounts predict different outcomes. Two explanations that agree in every context you can think of are the same explanation stated twice, and discovering that is itself a result: the distinction you were drawing does no work.

Returning to an earlier step is normal. Naming a mechanism often reveals that you generalized to the wrong class, and constructing a rival often exposes circularity that survived step 3.

Exercise

Pick one observation from the industry inputs.
Run it through the loop.

  1. State the initial, likely circular or too-specific, explanation, as a practitioner might state it.
  2. Generalize it to a class of things.
  3. Remove the circularity, name the mechanism.
  4. Propose at least one competing explanation for the same observation.
  5. Name a context in which the two explanations would predict different outcomes, your discriminating test.
  6. Check the account you end up with: is it testable, is it parsimonious, and where are its boundary conditions?
20:00

Step 5 is where most accounts fail. A pair of competing explanations that make identical predictions in every context you can think of redescribes the same idea twice rather than doing useful theoretical work. Lave & March (1993)’s Limited Time, Excellence, and Jealousy theories are held to exactly this standard: each is falsifiable against a different set of contexts.

Step 6 turns the quality checks from a list into a tool. Testability and parsimony are usually quick to judge. Boundary conditions take longer and repay the time, since an account whose limits you cannot state has usually claimed more than the observation supports.

Theorizing Your Brief

Our Expectations

You need to apply theorizing principles to deliver three core sections of your brief.

Knowledge Gap. We have discussed three kinds of knowledge gaps you can identify: an assumption that does not hold, a contradiction between findings, or an explanation that seems not to work (anymore). All three admissible kinds are theorizing results. A failing assumption is a mechanism that was asserted and never checked. A contradiction between findings is two competing explanations nobody has reconciled, and Bacharach (1989)’s criterion says the reconciliation runs through a boundary condition rather than through averaging the two. An explanation that seems not to work (anymore) is a mechanism whose scope was drawn too narrowly, in population or in time, the same fault the classroom example addressed by moving from athletes to any time-consuming activity.

The Chain. You are asked to close your Problem Framing with one paragraph running domain to phenomenon to gap to question, and the template warns that a link needing you present to explain it is not yet written down. Each link asserts that something follows from something else, which makes each link a small theory with the same failure mode you spent today removing. A circular link survives a read-aloud because it sounds like a reason: “the teams resist the system because they are resistant to new systems” passes until someone asks how the resistance was established independently of the refusal.

Role of Theory. Theory enters your review twice, and the template asks you to say what it does on each side.

Coming in, you are asked to classify the lens(es) you intend to use by Gregor (2006)’s type, because the type fixes what the lens can supply. Analysis (I) supplies vocabulary and categories, which become the a priori codes of your extraction scheme. Explanation (II) supplies a mechanism and its conditions, which is what lets you say why studies found what they found, and where a boundary condition might sit when they disagree. Prediction (III) supplies expected associations without a why, so its value is an expectation your findings can fail against. Explanation and prediction (IV) supplies both a coding scheme and a mechanism for reconciling contradictions. Design and action (V) supplies the criteria by which reported interventions get extracted and judged. Whatever you name here is what the Review Protocol will have to declare as its a priori codes in Phase 3.

Going out, you are asked which of the five types your review itself aims to produce, and what that commits you to: synthesising existing conceptualisations, surfacing competing causal mechanisms, or synthesising design principles.

The relation between the two sides is the part that gets skipped. A review applies its lens, extends it, or sets two lenses against each other so that the output is a boundary condition stating when each holds. The theory you bring and the theory you produce may be the same one, and then the question is what your review adds to it: a new construct, a relationship it did not specify, or a boundary condition it did not state. Two checks guard the relation. A lens cannot produce a type it does not reach, so a taxonomy needs either the primary studies or a second lens to supply causal logic. And a lens is doing work only when removing it would change which studies you include, how you code them, or how you reconcile two that disagree.

If you are still choosing between two candidate lenses, which the template permits, treat them as competing explanations and name the context in which they would lead you to read the same study differently. Two lenses that generate the same claims about your case are one lens with two citations.

Brief Check

Open the Problem Framing Brief drafts of your peer review groups and check for each:

  1. Knowledge Gap. Which of the three admissible gap types is theirs: an assumption that does not hold, a contradiction between findings, or an explanation that seems not to work (anymore)? If none of them fits, the gap is about publication counts.
  2. The Chain. Mark every link that explains an outcome by restating it, and name the mechanism it needs instead.
  3. Role of Theory. Classify their lens by Gregor’s type and name what it supplies. Does that type reach the type their review aims to produce?
  4. Removal test. Would dropping the lens change which studies they include, how they code them, or how they reconcile two that disagree?
  5. The relation. Is it stated at all: does the brief say whether the review applies the lens, extends it, or sets two against each other?
