Opening Remarks

Protocol Reproducibility

Today another team executes your protocol, without you in the room, and tells you where it fails.

Knowledge, Science and Epistemology named replicability as one of the principles a claim needs to count as scientific knowledge: a procedure documented well enough that someone outside the team can repeat it and obtain similar results. Your Review Protocol now covers the planning, search, and screening decisions from SLR I, SLR II, and SLR III. Today a team that did not write it reproduces its search and screening and reviews every section against the criteria your protocol is graded on.

Learning Outcomes

By the end of this unit, you will be able to:

  • Execute another team’s search strategy exactly as written and diagnose why your result diverges from their search log.
  • Apply another team’s screening criteria to their pilot sample and measure your agreement with their decisions.
  • Review a Review Protocol section by section against the assessment criteria, with specific and actionable comments.
  • Revise your own protocol against every comment you received and record each change in the revision log.

Review Object

Protocol So Far

Each team reviews the protocol sections prepared in SLR I to SLR III.

The upload due before this session contains the protocol draft with all sections from SLR I to SLR III, including the Search Strings appendix with the search log, and, as a separate file, the 20 records used in the pilot screening (titles and abstracts, without decisions). Teams may add the export of their final search runs (RIS or BibTeX); it allows reviewers to compare records as well as counts. The sections on data extraction, coding, and synthesis follow in SLR IV: Analysis and Synthesis and are not part of this review.

Reproduction pairings are fixed in advance and published on Moodle: within each table of three teams, Team A reproduces and reviews Team B’s protocol, Team B reviews Team C’s, and Team C reviews Team A’s.

Reproduction Rules

Reproduction tests the protocol as written.

  • Protocol only: work from the uploaded documents; no questions to the authors.
  • Exactly as written: same sources, strings, fields, limits, and criteria.
  • Compare against the log: hit counts per run and the test set, since the protocol records counts, not paper sets.
  • Log everything: every run and every screening decision, as the authors should have.

“No questions to the authors” mirrors the situation of every reader of a published review: nobody can ask the original team what they meant. An unwritten clarification the protocol needs in order to be repeatable is exactly the gap this exercise surfaces.

The protocol contains the search log with string, fields, limits, date, and hit count per run, plus the three to five key papers of the test set. Reproduction therefore compares hit counts and test-set retrieval. Where authors uploaded the export of their final run, reviewers can also compare records, for example the first 50 hits. Log your own runs in the format of the example search log from SLR II, so that the authors can compare your runs with theirs line by line.

Reproduction

Divergence Sources

Divergence between your result and the authors’ log has a limited set of causes.

  • Ambiguous terms: a search term or criterion that can reasonably be read two ways, and was.
  • Undocumented filters: a date range, language, document type, or field restriction applied but never written down.
  • Database differences: the same source searched on a different day, through a different interface, or with a different license.
  • Silent judgment calls: a screening decision that made sense to the authors at the time but was never turned into a written rule.

Three of the four causes stem from what was not written down. The fourth, database differences, cannot be removed, only documented: the search date and interface in the Databases and Sources table let a reader tell a database update from a protocol gap. When a divergence fits none of the four causes, name a fifth one; it is a finding for the authors as well.

Exercise: Search Reproduction

As a team, reproduce the search strategy of the protocol you were assigned.

  1. Run the authors’ string in at least one of their sources, with the fields and limits they state.
  2. Compare your hit count with the count in their search log.
  3. Check whether their test set (three to five key papers) appears in your result.
  4. Assess their string against PRESS: translation of the question, Boolean operators, index terms, free-text terms, spelling and syntax, limits and filters.
  5. Trace every divergence to one of the four causes or to a fifth one you name.
40:00

Steps 1 to 3 reproduce the search; step 4 reviews it. The PRESS guideline (McGowan et al., 2016) structures the peer review of electronic search strategies in six elements: the translation of the research question into concepts, the use of Boolean and proximity operators, subject headings or index terms, free-text terms with truncation and spelling variants, spelling, syntax, and line numbers, and the limits and filters applied. Teams that finish early check the complementary searches: is the snowballing plan specific about start set, tool, and stopping rule, and is the grey-literature decision justified?

Expect hit counts to differ slightly when the search date differs. A difference of a few records points to database updates; a difference of hundreds points to a field, limit, or term that the protocol does not state. A key paper missing from your result while the authors report it as found points to the same kind of gap.

Exercise: Screening Reproduction

As a team, apply the authors’ screening criteria to their pilot sample.

  1. Screen the authors’ 20 pilot records with their criteria, without looking at their decisions.
  2. Compare your decisions with theirs and compute po and Cohen’s kappa.
  3. For every disagreement, name the criterion and the wording that produced it.
25:00

This exercise repeats the pilot screening from SLR III across teams. The authors’ decisions on the 20 records are part of the Selection Process section; reviewers open it only after screening. A team that is new to the topic is the strictest test of a criterion: it has only the written rule. Low agreement between teams, even where the authors reached high agreement among themselves, shows that a criterion relies on a shared understanding inside the author team. The disagreements identify the criteria that need a definition, an example, or a stage assignment, and they belong in the written review.

Exercise: Debrief

Plenary, one sentence per team.

  1. Did your hit count match the authors’ log exactly, approximately, or not at all?
  2. What kappa did you reach with the authors’ screening decisions?
  3. Which divergence cause came up most often?

Reviewing the Protocol

Review Criteria

The review follows the assessment criteria of the Review Protocol, section by section.

The review questions condense the assessment criteria for the Review Protocol: executable by someone else; review type derived from the question and scope decisions argued; working definitions and documented search terms for each concept, including excluded near-terms; a search that operationalizes the question with plausible sources; screening criteria as rules a third party can apply, each with a stage and a rationale; and substantive, reasoned revisions in the log. The commitments for extraction, coding, and synthesis are not yet part of the protocol and are not reviewed today.

The two reproduction exercises supply evidence for the review: the search reproduction for the search strategy rows, the screening reproduction for the screening rows. A review that cites the reproduction result (“your string returned 412 records for us, against 187 in your log”) is harder to dismiss than one that states an impression.

Reviewer Craft

The craft from Peer-Review Workshop I applies to a new artifact.

  • Polite and specific: name the line, quote it, state what is missing.
  • Gem first: say what works before what fails.
  • Concise: one comment per issue.
  • Consistent: the verdict follows from the comments.

What changes is the object under review: a protocol’s decisions and their documentation, where Peer-Review Workshop I reviewed a research question’s argument chain. Calibrate on the example review before writing your own; it sets the expected level of specificity in advance.

Example Review

Comments from a review of an invented protocol show the expected level of specificity.

  • Review type and scope: “You name a qualitative systematic review, but your question asks which approaches exist and where they are studied, a what/where question that the alignment matrix assigns to a mapping or scoping review. Either reframe the question as how/why or change the review type.”
  • Concepts and search terms: “The concept ‘AI agent’ has no working definition, and the Concepts and Search Terms table lists no excluded near-terms. Your string retrieves multi-agent simulation papers from the 1990s; state whether ‘multi-agent system’ is excluded and why.”
  • Search strings: “Your protocol states ('AI agent' OR 'autonomous agent') AND trust, but your search log shows an added AND organizational term. Our run without it returned 412 records against your 187, and two of your five key papers were missing. Add the term to the written string.”
  • Screening criteria: “Criterion 3, ‘sufficiently related to the topic’, is no rule a stranger can apply. On your pilot sample we reached κ = .38 with your decisions; five of our seven disagreements concern this criterion.”
  • Quality appraisal: “You appraise with a checklist for experiments, but your corpus consists mainly of case studies. Name an instrument that fits qualitative designs, and state whether appraisal excludes or weights studies.”

Each comment names a specific line, quotes or paraphrases it, and states precisely what is missing. Three of the five cite evidence from the reproduction, which turns a reviewer’s opinion into a finding the authors can verify.

Exercise: Written Review

As a team, draft the review of the protocol you reproduced.

  1. Write one comment per row of the review criteria where you found an issue, in the format of the example review.
  2. Cite your reproduction results wherever they support a comment.
  3. Open with what works, and close with a verdict: minor or major revision.
20:00

The written draft is the Reviewer’s script for the roundtable. Teams that draft first present more specific findings and leave more time for the authors’ questions.

Exercise: Roundtable

Form the same tables of three teams as in Peer-Review Workshop I. Who reviews whom follows from who reproduced whom.

Each round has three roles: the Reviewer is the team that reproduced the protocol under discussion and presents its review, citing reproduction results; the Author is the team whose protocol it is, listening and asking clarifying questions; the Scribe, the third team at the table, writes the review down.

  1. Round 1 (15 min): Team B is Author, Team A is Reviewer, Team C is Scribe.
  2. Round 2 (15 min): Team C is Author, Team B is Reviewer, Team A is Scribe.
  3. Round 3 (15 min): Team A is Author, Team C is Reviewer, Team B is Scribe.

Every team reviews once, is reviewed once, and scribes once. Keep the Scribe’s notes and the Reviewer’s written review: your revision homework works from both.

Tables of four: add a fourth round and shorten every round to 11 minutes. Tables of two: skip the scribe role; the Reviewer hands over its written review instead.

45:00

The roundtable mirrors Peer-Review Workshop I, adapted for a review that rests on reproduction work. The Reviewer presents findings from work it has just completed: how its hit counts compared with the authors’ log, whether the test set was retrieved, what kappa the screening reproduction reached, which divergence cause explains each mismatch, and which protocol sections fall short of the review criteria, citing the specific line behind each comment, the standard set by the example review.

Exercise: Read-Aloud Debrief

Each Scribe reads one note from their table aloud. The team that received it says, in one word, whether it is actionable.

10:00

The debrief has the same function as in Peer-Review Workshop I: it surfaces within minutes whether the live feedback met the standard of being specific and constructive, publicly enough that a Scribe whose notes were too vague finds out immediately.

Homework

Revise your Review Protocol in light of the review you received.

  1. Tabulate every comment from the written review and the Scribe’s notes: comment, response, change.
  2. For every gap, either close it by making the decision explicit or justify why it remains a judgment call.
  3. Record every change in the revision log of the protocol, with version, date, section, and reason.

Tabulating Reviewer Comments

When authors revise a paper after review, a common practice is to tabulate every reviewer comment with three columns: the comment, the response, and the change made. It forces a response to every point, including the inconvenient ones. Apply the same discipline here: respond to each gap the reproducing team found in your revised protocol, and record the change in the revision log, where a general tidy-up would leave specific issues unaddressed.
