Opening Remarks

Synthesis Process

You now have a screened, appraised set of papers, the raw material for a review. Turning that material into a review is the work still ahead of you.

Search and screening determine which studies enter the review; synthesis determines what the review says about them. It turns a set of individually read papers into one defensible argument about what the field currently knows, and that argument is the contribution your review makes. This unit fixes how you extract data, code it, and synthesize it, and it prepares the last two sections of your Review Protocol.

Data Extraction

Extraction Form

An extraction form records the same fields from every included study (Kitchenham & Charters, 2007).

  • Bibliographic fields: reference, outlet, year.
  • Study fields: research method, sample, context, theory used.
  • Question fields: what your research question needs from every study, such as constructs, mechanisms, boundary conditions, findings.
  • Lens fields: the constructs of the lens you named in the brief.

What you choose to extract encodes a decision about what matters in these papers. An extraction form with a field for method and none for boundary conditions decides, before you have read a single finding closely, that method matters to your synthesis and boundary conditions do not.

Build the form from three sources: your research question (what would you need recorded from every paper to answer it?), the role you gave theory in your Problem Framing Brief (the constructs of a declared lens become fields and a priori codes), and your review type (a meta-analysis needs effect sizes and sample sizes; a mapping study needs classification fields; a theoretical review needs constructs and their relations). Generic templates miss the question fields, which are the ones the synthesis depends on.

Extraction Practice

The five reviews show how extraction is organized in practice.

  • Pilot: Martínez-Fernández et al. (2022) piloted their form on three papers, each extracted independently by all eight authors.
  • Split extraction: Rajapakse et al. (2022) and Garousi & Mäntylä (2016) divided the papers between two authors who then checked each other’s sheets.
  • Coding rules: King & He (2006) fixed rules for deriving correlations from reported statistics before coding.
  • Traceability: Garousi & Mäntylä (2016) stored a verbatim quote for every extracted factor.

Kitchenham & Charters (2007) recommend that data extraction be performed independently by two researchers, or by one researcher with a second checking a sample. Few reviews do this for every paper. Martínez-Fernández et al. (2022) piloted their extraction form on three seed papers with all eight authors and extracted the remaining papers singly, with weekly consistency meetings. Rajapakse et al. (2022) split 54 papers between two authors, which they present as following Kitchenham’s advice although each paper was extracted once. Garousi & Mäntylä (2016) split their sources in halves and peer-reviewed each other’s results; their spreadsheet stores a verbatim quote for every extracted item, which lets a reader trace each synthesized factor back to its source.

For your protocol, a pilot on a few papers by both authors, followed by split extraction with cross-checks, is a defensible minimum. Store the passage behind every extracted value; it makes the synthesis checkable.

Coding

Coding Modes

The three modes of theorizing from What is Theorizing apply to coding a corpus.

  • Deductive: code each paper against the categories of an existing framework or lens.
  • Inductive: let codes emerge from the papers and build categories bottom-up.
  • Abductive: start with a rough framework and revise it as the corpus surprises you.
  • Deductive coding fits when your brief named a lens you intend to apply or extend, and its constructs are specific enough to decide, for each paper, whether a category applies. Heyder et al. (2023) code deductively with a codebook derived from their framework.
  • Inductive coding fits young or conceptually unsettled domains, where a pre-existing framework would hide the disagreement you want to surface. Hund et al. (2021) follow the grounded-theory approach of Wolfswinkel et al. (2013) and let categories emerge.
  • Abductive coding starts with a priori codes from the lens and adds emergent codes where the corpus requires them. It is the most common mode in practice and usually the most accurate one to report.

The choice follows from two earlier decisions: your review type from SLR I, since a meta-analysis has little use for inductive coding and a scoping review little use for a rigid deductive scheme, and the role you gave theory in your brief, since a declared lens supplies the a priori categories deductive coding needs.

Codebook Anatomy

A codebook defines every code so that a second coder applies it the same way (DeCuir-Gunby et al., 2011).

  • Name: a short label.
  • Definition: what the code captures, with central terms defined.
  • Decision rule: when to apply it and when not, including borderline cases.
  • Example: a typical passage from a paper.
  • Origin: a priori (from the lens) or emergent (from the corpus).

The Draft Codebook appendix of the protocol asks for name, definition, decision rule, and example for every code you start with, marks which codes are a priori, and leaves space for codes that emerge during coding. DeCuir-Gunby et al. (2011) describe the same anatomy for interview data: a code is usable when a second coder, given only the codebook, applies it to the same passages. Heyder et al. (2023) follow the comparable format of Mayring, with definitions, anchor examples, and coding rules per category.

A decision rule does for a code what a criterion does for screening in SLR III: it turns a label into a rule a third party can apply. Codes without decision rules produce low agreement in the double-coding subsample, for the same reason vague screening criteria produce low kappa.

Example: Digital Innovation

Hund et al. (2021) coded 227 articles in three stages, following Wolfswinkel et al. (2013).

  1. Open coding: all four authors read 20 randomly selected articles and labeled excerpts in a joint workshop: 292 open codes.
  2. Axial coding: the first author related codes to categories and subcategories while coding the remaining articles, in consultation with two co-authors.
  3. Selective coding: a second workshop related the categories to main categories, until new excerpts revealed nothing new.

The result is a data structure, reported in the paper’s Appendix B, that traces excerpts to open codes, open codes to axial categories, and categories to five main themes: redefinition of boundaries, digital systems, digital innovation strategy, organizational determinants, and arising tensions. A reader can follow how a theme was built from the papers.

The example also shows what remains open. After the first workshop, one author coded most of the corpus, and the paper reports no agreement measure for that coding. Your protocol closes this gap with a double-coding subsample: both authors code the same papers independently, you report Cohen’s kappa (Cohen, 1960) or the share of agreement per code, and you resolve disagreements by a rule fixed in advance.

Concept Matrix

Concept-Centric Review

Webster & Watson (2002) distinguish two ways to organize a review.

  • Author-centric: study by study, in the order the papers were read.
  • Concept-centric: concept by concept, with findings grouped by what they are claims about.

An author-centric review walks through the literature study by study: “Smith (2020) found X. Jones (2021) found Y. Lee (2022) found Z.” A concept-centric review is organized around the concepts: “Regarding construct A, three studies find X, while two studies, conducted in a different context, find not-X.” The author-centric version mirrors the reading order and tells the reader what each study said; the concept-centric version tells the reader what the field knows, because it cannot be written without first grouping findings by what they are claims about.

The tool is a concept matrix: rows are included papers, columns are concepts or constructs. The columns come from two sources, the a priori codes of your lens and the categories that emerged while coding. Fill each cell with what the paper says about the concept, read the matrix by column, and write the synthesis from that reading. This is the Organization decision from SLR I in practice.

Example: Concept Matrix

A minimal matrix of three invented papers and two concepts shows the column-wise reading.

Read the matrix by column. The governance column tells a synthesis story: three papers report three different structures, from centralized to absent, which is a finding about the corpus. The adoption-driver column shows that the corpus disagrees on why organizations adopt at all, regulation, competition, or cost, which is the kind of disagreement the contingency analysis explains. Reading by row produces facts about single papers: “P1 has centralized governance and cites regulatory pressure” says nothing yet about what the corpus knows about governance.

Hund et al. (2021) present their findings in this concept-centric form: each of their five themes has its own table, with categories, definitions, and the supporting papers grouped by discipline.

Synthesis Techniques

Technique by Review Type

The synthesis technique follows from the review type chosen in SLR I.

  • Mapping and scoping reviews classify studies and count them by category, year, method, or venue; the result is a map. Martínez-Fernández et al. (2022) map SE approaches by SWEBOK area.
  • Qualitative systematic reviews use thematic synthesis: codes are grouped into descriptive themes and then into analytical themes that answer the review question (Thomas & Harden, 2008). Cruzes & Dybå (2011) adapt the steps for software engineering. Rajapakse et al. (2022) follow the thematic analysis of Braun & Clarke (2006).
  • Theoretical reviews use framework synthesis when a framework exists, as Heyder et al. (2023) do, or grounded theory when categories should emerge, as Hund et al. (2021) do following Wolfswinkel et al. (2013).
  • Meta-analyses pool effect sizes statistically and explain heterogeneity with moderator analyses, as King & He (2006) do.
  • Multivocal reviews synthesize thematically and keep a trace from each factor to its sources, as Garousi & Mäntylä (2016) do.

Narrative synthesis, a structured textual comparison, is the fallback when studies are too heterogeneous to pool and a thematic organization would lose their comparative structure. Name the technique in your protocol and in your paper.

Contradictions and Evidence

Contingency Factors

A good theory reconciles contradictory findings by naming the contingent factors behind them (Bacharach, 1989).

  • Compare: what differs between the studies: context, sample, operationalization, time?
  • Name: the factor that explains why both findings hold.
  • State: the boundary condition that follows.

You met this criterion in What is Theorizing: Bacharach (1989) expect a good theory to explain why one study found X and another found not-X. Your review is where you apply the criterion. When two papers disagree, reporting both and moving on, or treating the majority finding as the finding, leaves the contradiction unexplained. Ask what differs between the studies that could explain the difference. If you can name the contingency factor, you improve the field’s explanation, one of the three routes to contribution from Knowledge, Science and Epistemology.

King & He (2006) give a real example. Their moderator analysis splits TAM studies by user type and finds that students can serve as surrogates for professional users, but not for general users. Studies that seemed to disagree about the strength of TAM relationships turn out to differ in whom they sampled; user type is the contingency factor, and the boundary condition is a statement about which samples a TAM finding generalizes to.

Evidence Visibility

The protocol states how the synthesis shows which conclusions rest on many studies and which on few.

  • Counts: report the number of supporting studies per theme or concept.
  • Quality: connect conclusions to the appraisal from SLR III.
  • Contradictions: report disconfirming studies next to confirming ones.

Hund et al. (2021) report the number of supporting papers for each of their five themes, from 34 papers for the redefinition of boundaries to 124 for organizational determinants, and group them by discipline. Rajapakse et al. (2022) list the supporting papers for each challenge. Counts alone can mislead when many weak studies outweigh a few strong ones, which is why the appraisal decision from SLR III (exclude, weight, or describe) returns here: a conclusion supported by two well-appraised studies may deserve more confidence than one supported by ten opinion pieces.

Plausible factors include task criticality (low-stakes tasks versus decisions with consequences users must answer for), the transparency of the agent’s reasoning, user expertise, and the availability of a fallback when the agent fails. A strong answer states the boundary condition in testable form, for example: “Autonomy increases trust when the agent’s decisions can be inspected after the fact, and decreases it when they cannot.”

Synthetic Contribution

Contribution Threshold

A pattern describes what the literature says; explaining why it holds, and where it stops holding, makes it a contribution.

“Twelve of the fifteen papers in our corpus find X” is an observation. It becomes a contribution when you can say why, by naming a mechanism that would produce the regularity, and where the boundary is, by naming the conditions under which you would expect the pattern to break down, and checking whether the three papers that found not-X sit at that boundary. A synthesis is finished when it states a claim, with its boundary conditions, that the field did not have in this form before your review put the pieces together.
