Opening Remarks

Search Repeatability

The real test of your search: could someone else, given only your protocol, find the same papers you did?

Last session you decided what kind of review you are doing and fixed its scope. This session produces the first concrete artifact of executing that review: a search strategy specific enough that a stranger, given only what you wrote down, could run it and arrive close to where you did. You will test that claim in Peer-Review Workshop II, so what you write today needs to survive being handed to someone else.

Learning Outcomes

By the end of this unit, you will be able to:

  • Select and justify databases for a review that spans software engineering and information systems.
  • Derive concept blocks from your research question and build a Boolean search string from them.
  • Test a search string for sensitivity and precision.
  • Plan backward and forward snowballing and make a justified grey-literature decision.
  • Log a search so that someone else can repeat it.

Protocol Section

This unit prepares the Search Strategy section of your Review Protocol.

Today fills the Search Strategy section of your Review Protocol. In the SLR process (Kitchenham & Charters, 2007), the search is the first step of the conducting phase, after the planning decisions from SLR I and before selection, appraisal, extraction, and synthesis.

Written in Advance

A systematic search earns its credibility by being specified before it is run, so that the result set cannot steer the method. Refining the string over several runs is normal and expected. Each version is dated, logged with its hit count and the reason for the change, and kept in the log after it has been replaced. A paper that presents only the final string conceals how the corpus came about.

Writing the strategy down before executing it and keeping every version turns “we searched the literature” into a claim someone else can check.

Choosing Databases

Source Choice

No database covers everything. The question is which combination covers your topic, and whether you can justify it.

  • Coverage gaps: many IS journals and most conference papers are missing from the big index databases.
  • Different engines: the same string returns different results, because Boolean support, fields, and ranking differ.
  • Weak indexing: abstracts and index terms are often too poor to judge relevance.
  • Reproducibility: some search systems cannot be queried reproducibly at all.

Brocke et al. (2015) show that many IS journals and most conference proceedings are not indexed in the large multidisciplinary databases, and that the same search string behaves differently across engines because they differ in Boolean support, searchable fields, stop words, and ranking. Brereton et al. (2007) found software-engineering indexing databases inadequate for systematic reviews, with abstracts often too poor to judge relevance. Gusenbauer & Haddaway (2020) tested 28 academic search systems and found that several of them, Google Scholar among them, cannot be queried reproducibly at all.

Which databases you search operationalizes the Coverage decision from SLR I: claiming exhaustive coverage while searching a single narrow database is a contradiction the protocol needs to resolve.

Five Kinds of Sources

Sources differ in what they index and in the role they can play in a systematic search.

Principal sources are those you run your full Boolean string against and export from; they have to support nested Boolean logic, field restriction, and bulk export. Supplementary sources serve narrower purposes: checking whether a known paper exists, or following citations.

Google Scholar is no principal source: its results are not reproducible, its Boolean support is limited, and search strings are capped at 256 characters (Gusenbauer & Haddaway, 2020). It is useful for forward snowballing and for locating full texts.

Recommended Sources

Software engineering and information systems recommend different starting points for the search.

Software engineering

  • No single source suffices (Brereton et al., 2007).
  • IEEE Xplore and ACM DL as must-have portals (Zhang et al., 2011).
  • Scopus plus backward and forward snowballing as a hybrid strategy (Mourão et al., 2020).

Information systems

  • Start with leading journals and conferences, then go backward and forward (Webster & Watson, 2002).
  • Common sources: Web of Science, EBSCOhost, Scopus, AIS eLibrary, ProQuest, ACM, IEEE, ScienceDirect (Bandara et al., 2015).
  • Do not restrict the search to a few top journals; include conferences (Brocke et al., 2015).

Software engineering has empirically tested source lists: Brereton et al. (2007) list IEEE Xplore, ACM DL, Google Scholar, CiteSeer, Inspec, ScienceDirect, and Ei Compendex and conclude that no single source suffices; Zhang et al. (2011) identify IEEE Xplore and ACM DL as must-have portals; Mourão et al. (2020) show that Scopus combined with backward and forward snowballing performs well as a hybrid strategy.

IS has no comparable empirically tested list, so you justify your choice from coverage. Bandara et al. (2015) list the sources IS reviews commonly use, and the AIS Senior Scholars’ Basket1 names the field’s leading journals. Searching only the basket guarantees quality but not coverage: a young or interdisciplinary phenomenon may barely appear there yet, which is why Brocke et al. (2015) advise including conferences.

Six Questions per Source

Six questions decide whether a source belongs in your search.

  • Coverage: does it index the outlets where my topic is published, including conferences?
  • Query power: Boolean operators, nesting, truncation, phrase and field search?
  • Export: can I export all hits with abstracts (RIS, BibTeX, CSV)?
  • Reproducibility: will the same string return the same set next month?
  • Overlap: what does it add beyond the sources I already have?
  • Access: does HNU license it, and if not, what does that exclude?

Answer these six questions for each candidate source, and record the answers in the Databases and Sources table of your protocol. Report which databases you searched and why, and just as importantly, which ones you consciously did not search and what that choice costs.

A reasonable starting set for this term’s topics is OpenAlex (a free multidisciplinary index), AIS eLibrary (IS journals and conferences), and IEEE Xplore (SE and CS conferences). Add Scopus or ACM DL where your topic requires them.

Four Terms

Search string
The Boolean query typed into a database: synonyms joined by OR, concepts joined by AND.
Testing a string
Checking whether the string finds papers already known to be relevant (a test set), and how much noise it returns.
Snowballing
Finding more papers through citations: backward through the reference lists of included papers, forward through newer papers that cite them.
Grey literature
Material not published by commercial academic publishers, such as reports, white papers, preprints, blogs, and videos.

These four terms appear in the method section of almost every review. Their definitions suffice to recognize them in the search forensics exercise; the sections on string building, testing, snowballing, and grey literature explain how to do each of them well.

Repeatability Checklist

Nine items decide whether another team can repeat a search.

Use this checklist twice: on your assigned review in the search forensics exercise, and on your own protocol before you submit it. Each item corresponds to information another team needs to re-run your search; an item marked “no” is a point at which a replication would have to guess.

Building the Search String

Concept Blocks

Take your research question and identify its distinct concepts, usually two or three. For each concept, collect synonyms, related terms, and spelling variants, in both academic and practitioner vocabulary, since a phenomenon is rarely called the same thing in a journal article and in a trade press article. Combine the synonyms for one concept with OR into a block, and combine different blocks with AND.

Every extra AND block narrows the result. Before adding one, ask whether it removes more noise than relevant papers.

Worked Example: Concept Blocks

Start from the research question, “how do organizations use agentic AI to strengthen their cyber resilience,” and pull out its concepts: agentic AI, cyber resilience, and organization.

For each concept, collect synonyms. Agentic AI shows up as “AI agents,” “LLM-based agents,” or “autonomous agents,” terminology that has not stabilized yet because the phenomenon is young. Cyber resilience overlaps with “incident response,” “security operations,” and “cyber defense,” labels used by different communities for related activities. Truncation (*) catches word-family variants such as “resilience” and “resilient,” and the wildcard in organi?ation* catches British and American spellings.

Combine each block with OR, inside parentheses, and the blocks with AND:

("agentic AI" OR "AI agent*" OR "LLM-based agent*" OR "autonomous agent*") AND ("cyber resilien*" OR "incident response" OR "security operation*" OR "cyber defen*") AND (organi?ation* OR enterprise* OR firm* OR compan*)

Block C deserves a second look: many relevant papers never mention “organization” in their title or abstract, so it may cost more relevant papers than it removes noise. The block-by-block test, one of the four tests for a search string, shows whether to keep it.

PICOC Elements

PICOC is a checklist for finding the concepts in your question.

PICOC (Population, Intervention, Comparison, Outcome, Context) originated in evidence-based medicine as a way to structure a clinical question before searching the literature. Petticrew & Roberts (2006) adapted it for the social sciences, and Kitchenham & Charters (2007) adopted it for software engineering.

Not every element becomes a search block. Most strings are built from Intervention and Population, sometimes Outcome. Comparison and Context usually become eligibility criteria, because authors rarely name them in titles and abstracts; a block for “critical infrastructure” would drop every relevant paper that mentions its setting only in the method section.

The decomposition earns its value by forcing the question into specific parts, whether or not every slot ends up filled. An element present in the PICOC breakdown but missing from your research question is usually the ambiguity a reader trips on first.

PICOC Limits

PICOC fits some questions well and others poorly.

PICOC fits

  • Effectiveness questions: does X improve Y?
  • Systematic reviews of interventions (Reviews B, D)
  • Clearly named technologies or practices

PICOC struggles

  • No intervention: understanding a phenomenon (Review C)
  • Theory building, mapping a young field (Review A)
  • Concepts under many labels, or without a label yet

PICOC assumes an intervention whose effect you want to know. Many IS questions have none: a review that seeks to understand how organizations experience a phenomenon, or that maps what has been studied in a young field, has no Intervention or Comparison to fill in. Forcing the question into PICOC then produces empty or artificial slots. Alternative frameworks exist for these cases.

Framework Choice

Each question framework fits a question type, a theory type, and a review type.

Pick the framework that matches the alignment you argued in SLR I. SPIDER was developed for qualitative evidence synthesis (Cooke et al., 2012); it is more specific than PICO but can miss relevant studies (Methley et al., 2014). PCC is the standard framework for scoping reviews (Peters et al., 2020).

A framework structures the question; the string is built from its central elements only. Comparison, Context, Design, and Research type usually become eligibility criteria for SLR III: Screening and Appraisal, because authors rarely name them in titles and abstracts.

Term Sources

The search terms in each concept block come from the literature on your topic, collected from four sources.

  • Key papers: author keywords and title words of three to five papers you already know are relevant.
  • Thesaurus: index terms such as IEEE terms, ACM CCS, EBSCO subject terms, OpenAlex topics.
  • Pearl growing: run a narrow string, read the hits, harvest new terms, widen.
  • Neighboring fields: the same thing under other labels, such as “cyber resilience,” “business continuity,” “security operations.”

Search terms come from the literature. Start with the author keywords and title words of three to five key papers you already know are relevant. Check the index terms these papers carry in each database. Run a narrow string, read the hits, and harvest new terms (pearl growing). Look for the same phenomenon under the labels of neighboring fields.

Record what you leave out. “Multi-agent system*” brings in decades of agent research from before large language models; excluding it is a scope decision, and it belongs in the “near-terms excluded” column of the Concepts and Search Terms table with a reason.

The key papers you collect here become your test set: the final string must find them.

Platform Syntax

Scopus

TITLE-ABS-KEY ( ( "agentic AI" OR "AI agent*" OR "LLM-based agent*" )
  AND ( "cyber resilien*" OR "incident response" OR "security operation*" ) )
  AND PUBYEAR > 2019

OpenAlex (web search with title and abstract filter)

("agentic AI" OR "AI agent" OR "LLM-based agent")
  AND ("cyber resilience" OR "incident response" OR "security operations")

The logic of a string stays the same across databases; the syntax does not:

  • Field codes differ: TITLE-ABS-KEY in Scopus, TS= (topic) in Web of Science, a title and abstract filter in OpenAlex.
  • Truncation differs: Scopus uses *, while OpenAlex stems words automatically and offers no * in its web search.
  • Field restriction to title, abstract, and keywords narrows a search that returns too many irrelevant hits, but it narrows coverage too; document the tradeoff.

Adapt the syntax per database, keep the logic, and log each version verbatim in the Search Strings appendix.

Testing the String

Sensitivity and Precision

Two measures describe how well a search string performs (Zhang et al., 2011).

Sensitivity (recall)

relevant found ÷ all relevant Too low: the string is too narrow.

Precision

relevant found ÷ all found Too low: the string is too broad.

A string can fail in three ways: too narrow, too broad, or aimed at the wrong literature. Sensitivity measures the first, precision the second. In a systematic review, sensitivity comes first: a study the search misses cannot be screened back in, while an irrelevant hit costs only the time to read its title.

Since “all relevant” papers are unknown, sensitivity cannot be computed directly. It is estimated with a test set of papers known to be relevant. A string that returns 50,000 hits is unusable for screening; a string that returns 12 hits for a phenomenon you know has substantial literature probably contains an overly narrow term or an accidentally restrictive field limitation.

Four Tests

Four tests check a string before you run the full search.

  • Test set: do all three to five key papers appear? Aim for 70 to 80 % or more.
  • Precision sample: screen the first 50 hits by title and abstract. Fewer than about 1 in 10 relevant means too broad.
  • Block by block: compare hits for A, A AND B, A AND B AND C. Which block cuts the most, and does it cut relevant papers?
  • Miss analysis: for every missed key paper, find the cause: missing term, other label, not indexed.

Run all four tests before the full search:

  1. Test set: check whether the key papers appear. A larger test set, a quasi-gold standard built by hand-searching a few venues (Zhang et al., 2011), gives a better estimate; aim for 70 to 80 % or more.
  2. Precision sample: screen the first 50 hits by title and abstract. If fewer than about 1 in 10 are relevant, the string is too broad.
  3. Block by block: record the hits for block A alone, A AND B, and A AND B AND C. The block that cuts the most deserves a check: does it also cut key papers?
  4. Miss analysis: for every missed key paper, find out why. A missing term means fixing the string; a different label means adding a synonym; a paper missing from the database means adding a source or catching it by snowballing.

In addition, let another team read your string. Peer review of search strategies follows an established guideline in medicine (PRESS, McGowan et al., 2016); in this course, Peer-Review Workshop II plays that role.

Snowballing and Grey Literature

Snowballing

Citation search complements keyword search in two directions (Wohlin, 2014).

  1. Define the start set: included papers, diverse in authors and venues.
  2. Screen candidates with the same criteria as the database search.
  3. Repeat on newly included papers until an iteration adds nothing.
  4. Log per iteration: direction, tool, date, candidates, included.

Webster & Watson (2002) recommend complementing keyword search with two citation-based strategies:

  • Backward snowballing: examine the reference lists of the papers you included, to locate earlier, foundational work that may use different terminology than your string anticipated.
  • Forward snowballing: use a citation database (OpenAlex, Google Scholar, IEEE Xplore, ACM DL) to find later papers that cite the ones you included, catching recent work that builds on your core papers but may not share their keywords.

Wohlin (2014) turns this into a procedure. The start set consists of included papers, chosen to be diverse in authors and venues so that the snowball does not stay inside one research group’s citation network. Candidates are screened with the same inclusion and exclusion criteria as the database results. Each newly included paper enters the next iteration, until an iteration adds nothing. Every iteration is logged with direction, tool, date, number of candidates, and number included.

Together, the two directions catch papers that a keyword search systematically misses: older work using since-abandoned terminology, and newer work using terminology that has not stabilized yet. For a young phenomenon such as agentic AI, the second failure mode is often the larger one.

Grey Literature

Garousi et al. (2019) sort grey literature into three tiers by the control over its outlet.

Types

  • 1st tier, high outlet control: books, government and industry reports, white papers, preprints.
  • 2nd tier, moderate control: annual reports, news articles, presentations, videos, Q&A sites.
  • 3rd tier, low control: blogs, e-mails, tweets, forum posts.

Include it when …

  • practice moves faster than publication,
  • practitioner experience is the evidence you need,
  • academic literature is thin or biased toward positive results.

Garousi et al. (2019), building on Adams et al. (2017), sort grey literature into three tiers by the degree of control over the outlet and the known expertise of the producer. First-tier sources, such as government reports from agencies like ENISA or NIST, have high outlet control; third-tier sources, such as blog posts, have almost none.

For a field as young as agentic AI, the peer-reviewed literature lags behind the phenomenon. Restricting a search to peer-reviewed venues biases a review toward what was established and slow-moving enough to survive a full review cycle (Brocke et al., 2015). Working papers, preprints, and credible practitioner reports may be the only place where recent evidence exists.

Preprints on arXiv are the hard case for this term’s topics: scholarly in form, but not yet peer-reviewed. Decide whether to include them and justify the decision in your protocol.

Grey Literature Search

Grey literature needs its own rules for searching, stopping, and appraising.

Search

  • Google in a private window, location noted
  • Agencies: BSI, ENISA, NIST
  • Vendor reports and blogs (lower tier)

Stop

  • Theoretical saturation
  • Bounded effort
  • Evidence exhaustion

Appraise

  • Authority of the producer
  • Methodology and objectivity
  • Date, novelty, impact, outlet type

Grey literature has to be searched with the same transparency as everything else (Garousi et al., 2019):

  • Search: use web search in a private window and note the location, since results are personalized; reuse your concept blocks as simpler strings. Search the websites of relevant agencies, such as BSI (German Federal Office for Information Security), ENISA (EU Agency for Cybersecurity), and NIST (US National Institute of Standards and Technology). Vendor reports and blogs count as lower-tier sources.
  • Stop: web searches have no natural end, so define a stopping rule in advance: theoretical saturation (no new concepts appear), bounded effort (for example, the first n result pages), or evidence exhaustion (nothing relevant left).
  • Appraise: since grey sources have not been peer-reviewed, assess the authority of the producer, the methodology and objectivity of the source, and its date, novelty, impact, and outlet type.

Kitchenham et al. (2023) take the opposite position: grey material should be handled with guidelines for primary studies and kept out of secondary studies. Compare Review E, which includes 52 grey sources, with Review C, which excludes grey literature entirely. Both positions are defensible, provided the protocol states which grey sources are included and how they were found and appraised.

Documentation

Logging Requirements

Log every search while you run it, in enough detail for another team to repeat it.

  • Sources: every database, platform, and interface; outlets searched by hand; citation searching.
  • Strategy: full string per source, verbatim; fields, limits, filters with reasons; revision log.
  • Timing and numbers: date of every search; hits per source; duplicates removed, with method and tool.
  • Quality: how the string was tested; peer review of the strategy; updates and re-runs.

PRISMA-S (Rethlefsen et al., 2021) lists sixteen items a review should report about its search, condensed here into four groups. Brocke et al. (2015) make the same demand for IS: a search that is not documented cannot be evaluated.

Keep the log during the search. It becomes the Search Strings appendix of your protocol, the raw material for the method section of your paper, and the basis for the PRISMA flow diagram in SLR III: Screening and Appraisal.

Example: Search Log

Every run of the search goes into the log, including discarded runs.

Export every final run (RIS or BibTeX) with the date in the file name, for example openalex_2026-12-01_run2.csv. Keep discarded runs in the log: they show how the string was tested. Run 1 found only three of five key papers; the miss analysis led to two added terms in run 2, which found all five at an acceptable precision of 9 in 50.

Example: Snowballing Log

Snowballing iterations and grey-literature searches are logged in the same way.

Together with the database log, these numbers fill the PRISMA flow diagram in your paper.

Homework

Complete the Search Strategy section of your Review Protocol.

  1. Concepts and Search Terms: the table complete, with blocks, working definitions, terms, term sources, and excluded near-terms.
  2. Databases and Sources: the table with at least three sources, one line each on what the source adds.
  3. Search Strings: final string per source, test-set result, precision sample, and what you changed.
  4. Complementary Searches: snowballing plan (start set, tool, stopping rule) and a justified grey-literature decision.
  5. Search Strings appendix: search log of all runs, including discarded ones.

Self-check with the repeatability checklist before you submit. The complete protocol draft is due for Peer-Review Workshop II.
