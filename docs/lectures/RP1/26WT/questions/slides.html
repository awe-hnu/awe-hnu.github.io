Opening Remarks

Question Viability

Once you have identified an effective topic, you need to formulate research questions and propose a plan to address them.

This challenge is much more difficult than learning methods and theories, largely because it is undefined and highly contextual, without a fixed structure to rely on (Recker, 2021). The research question is the central element that shapes, and gets refined throughout, your whole review.

Effective Topics left you with three things: a domain, a phenomenon, and a problem with the existing knowledge about it. This session does not rebuild them. It derives the fourth element, the question itself, and uses it as a test on the first three: check the chain you wrote, create the question it forces, and rework whichever end fails.

Learning Outcomes

By the end of this unit, you will be able to:

  • Derive a research question as the logical conclusion of your domain, phenomenon, and knowledge-problem chain, and rework whichever end fails when no question follows.
  • Strengthen a weak knowledge gap, choosing between a hook, one of four defensible gap types, and a problematization of the literature’s assumptions.
  • Classify a question as Type 1 or Type 2, and check it against six quality principles and the common formulation faults.

Main Problem

Four Symptoms

Recker (2021) outlines four primary problems that are indicative of a research question being inadequate.

The “monologuing” problem, the “so what” problem, the “solving the world” problem, and the “multitude” problem.

  • The “monologuing” problem: you cannot state what research question you are tackling unless you engage in a five-minute monologue, you have not grasped the essence of the problem yet.
  • The “so what” problem: you cannot tell why answering the research question matters to anyone.
  • The “solving the world” problem: your question has value, but cannot be answered given your resource constraints.
  • The “multitude” problem: you ask (too) many questions instead of one.

Further categories of inappropriate research questions are (Recker, 2021):

  • Obvious questions: “Are there challenges in using information technology?” Of course there are. Obvious questions have answers to which everyone would agree.
  • Irrelevant questions: “What is the influence of weather on the salaries of technology professionals?” There is no reason to believe there is any influence whatsoever.
  • Absurd questions: “Is the earth flat after all?” Absurd questions have answers to which everyone would agree.
  • Definitional questions: “Is technology conflict characterised by disagreement?” The answer is simply a matter of creating a concept that says it does. A definition is a form of description. It does not count as research.
  • Affirmation questions: “Can a decision-support tool be developed to facilitate decision-making for senior retail executives?” Yes. Trivially.

Motivating Your Question

Logical Conclusion

A research question is the logical, necessary conclusion to a set of arguments (Recker, 2021).

Problem domain + important phenomenon + problem with the available knowledge = research question

We discussed the first three in Effective Topics. The research question is what follows from these.

  • Check the chain you have.
  • Create the question it forces.
  • Rework whichever end fails.

Asking a research question is the logical, necessary, and inevitable conclusion to a set of arguments (Recker, 2021). Those arguments establish an important problem domain, containing an important phenomenon that deserves attention, which relates to an important problem with the available knowledge about that type of phenomenon. Given all three, the question is not chosen. It is what remains to be asked.

That has a practical consequence. In Effective Topics we discussed the first three elements. Here we discuss how to derive the fourth and how to use it as a test on the other three.

  • Check. Read your domain, phenomenon, and knowledge problem back as one argument. Each step has to narrow the one before it, and the knowledge problem has to be about the phenomenon you just named rather than about the domain in general.
  • Create. Write the question that argument forces, in one sentence. If several questions compete for the slot, the chain is still too broad. If none presents itself, the knowledge problem is not yet a problem.
  • Rework. A question that does not follow is a finding about the chain rather than about your phrasing. Three failures are common. The knowledge problem is stated as an absence rather than as something wrong, which the next section repairs. The phenomenon is too broad for any single question to address. Or the question answers something the chain never raised, which usually means the question was written first and the chain reverse-engineered to fit it.

Expect to run the loop more than once. The chain and the question settle together rather than in sequence, and a brief that reads as though the question arrived last is usually the one where it did.

A brief example

Organizations invest heavily in new IT and seek benefits from those investments. Many of those benefits never materialize, because employees do not use the technologies. The literature has studied why individuals accept new technologies, but not why they reject them, and that is a problem. The question follows without being chosen: why do people reject new technologies? (Cenfetelli & Schwarz, 2011)

Example: Agile Practices

Problem domain

Organizations increasingly adopt agile software development practices to improve project outcomes and developer productivity, with 71% of organizations now using agile practices in their software development lifecycle.

Important phenomenon

Despite this widespread adoption, agile projects continue to suffer from alarmingly high failure rates (50-96%), often attributed to human-related challenges where developers experience imbalances between the team-enacted use of agile practices and their individual needs.

Problem with the available knowledge

The literature to date has implicitly assumed that developer needs are perfectly reflected in team-enacted agile practices, overlooking the potential for incongruence between team practices and personal requirements.

Research questions

How do daily congruence and incongruence in the team-enacted versus individually needed use of agile practices affect developer well-being? Additionally, how pronounced is the influence of this congruence and incongruence among frequent (versus infrequent) team feedback-seeking developers? (Benlian et al., 2025)

This moves from the broad agile-adoption domain to the specific congruence phenomenon, identifies a clear knowledge gap, an untested assumption about alignment, and poses a research question that tests it directly.

Example: Social Engineering

Problem domain

Cybersecurity threats represent a critical organizational challenge, with digital system dependence creating vulnerabilities that extend beyond technical safeguards to human-centered attacks. Attackers increasingly bypass technical defenses by targeting the “easier to breach” human element through social engineering (SE) attacks. The urgency has intensified with AI advances enabling highly scalable, personalized, and convincing attacks such as automated phishing, deepfake-based impersonation, and adaptive social manipulation.

Important phenomenon

Social engineering attacks represent a distinct socio-technical phenomenon where attackers exploit human vulnerabilities through social interaction rather than technical system breaches, relying on trust, deception, and psychological influence.

Problem with the available knowledge

Current understanding of social engineering suffers from both definitional fragmentation (50 unique definitions identified) and explanatory inconsistency, with dual-process theories providing conflicting evidence about whether peripheral or central processing creates vulnerability.

Research questions

  • RQ1: What defines social engineering? Addresses the definitional fragmentation limiting theoretical development.
  • RQ2: How does social engineering unfold? Resolves the explanatory contradictions by systematically synthesizing SE mechanisms across psychological, cognitive, affective, and contextual conditions.

This moves from the broad cybersecurity domain to the specific SE phenomenon, identifies clear knowledge problems (fragmentation and contradiction), and poses research questions that directly address these gaps.

Example: Open-Source Takeover

Problem domain

Commercial software rests on open-source dependencies that few organizations audit and fewer maintain. A single widely linked library propagates to every downstream consumer, so compromising one project reaches the whole supply chain. The backdoor planted in XZ Utils (CVE-2024-3094) reached the compression library that Linux distributions link into their SSH daemons, and was caught by chance weeks before it would have shipped in stable releases.

Important phenomenon

The malicious commit was the last step of a campaign that ran for roughly two years. Sock-puppet accounts applied coordinated pressure on an isolated, overloaded maintainer, while a second identity established itself as a conspicuously helpful co-maintainer and was eventually granted release authority. The attack operated on the project’s social process; the code was only the payload.

Problem with the available knowledge

Two assumptions are worth challenging. First, research on supply chain attacks takes the malicious artifact as the unit of analysis and treats the preceding social campaign as narrative background, which leaves the governance phases that made the compromise possible untheorized. Second, the literature treats such campaigns as expensive, artisanal, and therefore rare. Agent frameworks lower the cost of operating sustained multi-account personas, and if that holds, the rarity assumption underpinning current detection posture no longer does.

Research questions

  • RQ1: What phases does a hostile takeover of an open-source project pass through? Builds the process model that artifact-centered research has not produced.
  • RQ2: What socio-technical behavioral indicators emerge before malicious code is submitted? Identifies observable signals in the phases the model names.

These are early ideas rather than a finished motivation. The knowledge problem still needs exploratory scoping before the questions can be narrowed, and the evidence base is mostly incident reports rather than peer-reviewed work.

Use this example to contrast the two question-generation strategies you will meet in the next section. The weak version of this topic is gap-spotting: “nobody has studied the social side of the XZ incident yet.” The version above names assumptions instead, that the commit is the right unit of analysis and that campaigns are too costly to run at scale, and asks what follows if they do not hold. That is the difference between an absence and a knowledge problem.

A phase model is a legitimate output for a review-only semester. You are synthesizing a sequence and its conditions across incident reports, open-source governance research, and the social engineering literature, which lands in Gregor’s analysis and explanation territory rather than requiring primary data of your own.

Behavioral indicators point forward to semester 2 and 3. A monitoring instrument that reads interaction patterns and discourse dynamics in a project’s issue tracker is a plausible candidate there, but note the trap on the Four Symptoms slide: “can a monitoring tool be built?” is an affirmation question, and the answer is trivially yes. The researchable form asks what design principles such an instrument should follow, and which indicators carry enough signal to justify acting on them.

On the evidence base, He, Miller, et al. (2026) and He, Agarwal, et al. (2026) both document how AI-assisted contribution changes the volume and review burden in software projects, which is the closest peer-reviewed footing currently available for the cost argument in the second assumption.

Problematization

Gap vs. Hook

The gap argues that something has not been done.

The hook finds a problem that someone cares about.

A defensible gap carries its own hook. Four do:

  • a contradiction in the literature, inconsistent observations or competing explanations;
  • a missing account for a specific phenomenon, understanding, or context;
  • a practice puzzle that matters and that the literature does not address;
  • misleading literature, resting on outdated or false assumptions.

“Nobody has studied X” is a statement about publication counts, and it motivates nothing: a reader who does not already care about X has been given no reason to start. The hook is the other half of the job, the reason the absence matters to someone.

The fact that they carry a hook inside them is what makes the following four kinds of gap defensible.

  • A contradiction in the literature: two studies observe the same thing and report incompatible findings, or two theories explain it in ways that cannot both hold. The hook is that the field cannot currently say which is right.
  • A missing account for a specific phenomenon, understanding, or context: not that nothing has been written, but that what has been written does not reach this case, together with a statement of why this case matters.
  • A practice puzzle: practitioners face something consequential that the literature does not address. Your industry inputs are the obvious source, and this is the gap type most teams here will have.
  • Misleading literature: the work exists, and rests on assumptions that are outdated or were never true. This one shades directly into problematization, which the rest of this section develops.

Match your own knowledge problem against the four before going further. A knowledge problem that fits none of them is usually a publication-count gap in better clothes, and the loop sends you back to rework it.

Weak Reasoning

“There is a gap in the literature” is the weakest possible reason to ask a question.

Ask which assumption might not hold instead. Alvesson & Sandberg (2011) name five types worth challenging.

  • In-house: a specific claim within a school of thought, on its own terms.
  • Root-metaphor: the field’s guiding image for a phenomenon.
  • Paradigmatic: a tradition’s underlying ontological or epistemological commitments.
  • Ideological: the political, moral, or interest-laden framing.
  • Field: assumptions shared across competing schools of thought.

Everything so far has trained you to spot a problem with existing knowledge, a gap, and to build a question that fills it. Alvesson & Sandberg (2011) argue this default mode, gap-spotting, systematically under-produces interesting research, because it accepts the assumptions of the existing literature and merely extends its boundaries. You end up with incrementally more of the same conversation rather than a new one.

Problematization is the alternative: instead of asking “what hasn’t been studied yet,” you ask “what assumption is this literature making that might not hold?” (Alvesson & Sandberg, 2011) identify several types of assumptions worth challenging:

  • In-house assumptions: challenging a specific claim or finding within a school of thought, on its own terms.
  • Root-metaphor assumptions: challenging the underlying image or metaphor a field uses to think about a phenomenon (e.g., is “adoption” really the right metaphor for how organizations take up new technology, or does it presuppose a passivity that isn’t there?).
  • Paradigmatic assumptions: challenging the deeper ontological or epistemological commitments a whole research tradition shares.
  • Ideological assumptions: challenging the political, moral, or interest-laden assumptions embedded in how a phenomenon is framed.
  • Field assumptions: challenging assumptions shared across multiple, otherwise competing, schools of thought.

Notice how this connects to what you already did in Synthesizing Insights: a genuine contradiction between two practitioner accounts is often evidence that a field’s assumption doesn’t hold in practice, which is exactly the kind of observation problematization asks you to build a question from, rather than a gap you noticed by counting what has and hasn’t been published.

Gap-Spotting vs. Problematization

Neither strategy is automatically better. A gap-spotting question, done well, is still a legitimate and often more tractable contribution for a semester-length review. But a topic that only survives as a gap (“nobody has looked at X in context Y yet”) is fragile, someone else can fill the same gap. A topic that survives as a problematization (“the field assumes X, but our evidence suggests otherwise”) is harder to write, but harder to make redundant too.

Question Quality

Question Types

Type 1: What, who, and where?
Type 2: How and why?

Type 1 explores and describes.
Type 2 explains.

Type 2 questions usually succeed Type 1 questions.

Research questions are typically one of two types, based on the issues they address (Recker, 2021).

Type 1
“What,” “who,” and “where” questions explore or describe, and fit where little knowledge exists yet. At the onset of the Covid-19 pandemic the first task was to establish what the virus looked like, what it did, what its genetic structure was, where it occurred, and who it could infect.
Type 2
“How” and “why” questions are explanatory, seeking the causal mechanisms at work in a particular phenomenon. Once the virus had been described, the question became how it infects people, which is what treatments and vaccines had to act on.

Type 2 questions usually succeed Type 1 questions, because explaining a phenomenon is difficult before it has been systematically described. A Type 2 question asked over a literature that has not yet done the Type 1 work is a question your review cannot answer, and noticing that is often itself the finding.

The classification carries well beyond this unit. In What is Theory it decides which of Gregor’s five types your question aims at, a Type 1 question pointing at analysis and a Type 2 question at explanation, or at explanation and prediction. In SLR I it becomes one of the three axes your review type has to align with. Settle it here, in one word, and carry it forward.

Six Principles

Recker (2021) proposes six principles for a question that focuses a study and guides how it is conducted:

  • Short: one line of text, 1.5 at most.
  • Feasible: you can observe every concept you use, reach the sources, and do the work in the time you have.
  • Interesting: you can sustain your own motivation for it across a full semester.
  • Novel: an answer will confirm previous findings, refute them, or add new ones.
  • Ethical: pursuing and answering it violates no principle of research conduct and puts no one at risk.
  • Relevant: the question and its answer inform scientific knowledge, industry practice, and future research.

Feasibility and ethics are the two that teams skip, and they are the two that cannot be repaired late. Feasibility for a review means something specific: the literature you would need has to exist, be findable with a search string you can write, and be readable in the weeks you have. A question whose evidence base is mostly blog posts and incident reports fails here even when it is the most interesting question in the room.

Short is a diagnostic rather than a style rule. A question that will not fit on one line is usually two questions, or a question still carrying its own motivation inside it.

Formulation Rules

  • No question answerable with yes or no.
  • No direct practical questions: “How can I improve X?” asks for a solution rather than for knowledge.
  • No infinite lists: “What factors influence…” gives no focus.
  • One question per sentence. An “and” or an “or” usually hides a second question.
  • “To what extent” commits you to measurement in numbers.

Research cannot answer a practical question directly. To address “how can we improve onboarding,” focus instead on what you do not understand about the phenomenon and investigate what is actually going on in it; the practical advice follows from the understanding rather than standing in for it.

Each question has to be a separate sentence, written as a question, with a question mark. The “and”/“or” test catches most violations, and Strich et al. (2021)’s two sub-questions show the correct handling: a genuine second question gets its own sentence rather than being folded into the first.

Many research questions relate variables to one another. Writing y for the variable to be explained and x for the variables assumed to influence it, the usual shapes are “How is x related to y?”, “What is the role of x for y?”, “Why does x influence y?”, and “To what extent does x influence y?”

Three cautions on those shapes. “To what extent” requires statistical evaluation and measurement in numbers, so it rarely suits qualitative work. For empirical work you need at least two precisely formulated variables, each phrased so that you can picture how it would be measured and where the data would come from. And a variable that expresses an activity or a process implies a longitudinal study, with several measurement periods, which usually exceeds what a master’s project can carry.

Guiding Questions

Recker (2021, pp. 35–36) proposes a number of guiding questions that can help you find a good research question, e.g.:

  • Do you know in which field of research your research question resides?
  • Do you have a firm understanding of the body of knowledge in the field?
  • What are important open research questions or unsolved problems in the field that scientists agree on?
  • What areas need further exploration?
  • Could your study fill an important gap in knowledge?
  • Has your proposed study been done before? If so, is there room for improvement or expansion?
  • Is the timing right for the question to be answered?
  • Who would care about obtaining an answer to the question?

  • Whether and how do conversational style and reciprocation triggers influence user information disclosure? (Adam & Benlian, 2024)

Take the questions one at a time. Type first, then the principle or rule each one strains against.

Rix et al. (2025), on antecedents to the valuation of human- versus algorithm-created products. Type 1 shading into Type 2: “antecedents” asks what, while the comparison implies a why. Short, feasible, novel, and relevant. The one strain is the infinite-list rule, since “antecedents” is a factor question; it survives because the two classes are named in advance, consumer-related and product-related, rather than left open.

Adam & Benlian (2024), on conversational style and reciprocation triggers. “Whether and how” packs two questions into one sentence: a Type 1 existence check and a Type 2 mechanism question. Splitting them is the fix, and that is the one-question-per-sentence rule doing its work. Otherwise short, feasible and relevant.

Benlian et al. (2025), on daily congruence in agile practice use. The cleanest of the five. Type 2, two precisely formulated variables, a single outcome, a bounded context, and within the length limit despite its apparent heft. Note that “daily congruence” is a process variable, which commits the original study to a diary design; for your review that is useful, since it tells you what kind of primary studies to look for.

Strich et al. (2021), on substitutive AI and professional role identity. Two Type 2 questions, correctly given separate sentences rather than joined with “and” inside one. Strong on significance and novelty. Watch feasibility: both questions ask about adaptation over time, so the evidence base is longitudinal and qualitative, and thinner than for the others.

Widjaja & Gregory (2020), on IT support for monitoring architectural complexity. The hardest case, and worth the time. It aims at design and action. It comes close to the practical-question rule, and is rescued by asking what support should be provided rather than how one organization should fix its architecture. It clearly fails Short, running well past 1.5 lines, and the nested phrasing, complexity of monitoring complexity, is what pushes it over.
