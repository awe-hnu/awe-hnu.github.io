Opening Remarks

Screening Decisions

Your search returned hundreds of records. Most of them do not belong in your review. Can you say why, in writing, before you decide?

Search produces candidates; screening decides which candidates become evidence. A review earns the label systematic when every keep-or-discard decision follows explicit, written rules that another person can apply and reach the same result. This unit turns the corpus from SLR II: Search Strategy into a set of included studies and fixes how you judge whether each study is good enough to inform your synthesis.

Learning Outcomes

By the end of this unit, you will be able to:

  • Write inclusion and exclusion criteria as rules that a third party can apply, each with a stage and a rationale.
  • Distinguish question-derived criteria from resource constraints and report both honestly.
  • Run a pilot screening with two independent screeners and measure their agreement with Cohen’s kappa.
  • Select a quality-appraisal approach that fits your review type and decide whether appraisal excludes, weights, or describes studies.
  • Report the selection process in a PRISMA flow diagram.

Selection Criteria

Practical Screen

Selection criteria are the practical screen between search results and evidence (Okoli, 2015).

  • Derived from the question: each criterion follows from the research question and the scope you fixed in SLR I.
  • Written before screening: fixed in the protocol and piloted before the full screening (Kitchenham & Charters, 2007).
  • Applied by rule: two people applying a criterion to the same record reach the same decision.

Okoli (2015) call the step from search results to included studies the practical screen: an explicit decision about which studies the review considers at all, separate from the later judgment about how good each study is. Kitchenham & Charters (2007) require that selection criteria follow from the research question, are written into the protocol before screening, and are piloted on a sample of records before the full screening begins.

A criterion is specific enough when two people, applying it independently to a paper neither has seen before, make the same decision. If two reasonable readers could disagree about a paper, the criterion needs a sharper wording, a definition of its central term, or an example of a borderline case.

Criterion Types

Selection criteria come in three types, each reported differently.

Question-derived criteria follow directly from what the research question is about: the phenomenon, the population, the setting. Design criteria follow from the review type: a primary-study review excludes other reviews, which it may use instead as sources of primary studies or as a test set. Resource criteria follow from what the team can handle: language, access, publication type, sometimes outlet rankings.

Resource criteria are legitimate, but they have to be reported as constraints, together with the bias they may introduce. “Only English-language papers” shifts the corpus toward Anglophone research; “only peer-reviewed papers” removes the most recent work on a fast-moving phenomenon; an outlet-ranking filter, which Heyder et al. (2023) use and describe as quality screening, removes studies on the basis of where they appeared.

Criterion Anatomy

Each criterion in the Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria table has four parts.

  • Rule: a testable statement about the record, with central terms defined.
  • Direction: include or exclude.
  • Stage: title and abstract, or full text.
  • Rationale: one sentence that defends the boundary.

A criterion that needs the full text to decide belongs to the full-text stage.

The Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria table of the protocol template asks for four columns per criterion. The rule states what a record must or must not show, with every central term defined: if your criterion mentions “AI agents,” the protocol says what counts as an agent. The direction says whether the rule includes or excludes. The stage says where the rule is applied: criteria about topic and setting can often be decided from title and abstract, while criteria about research design or the type of contribution usually need the full text. The rationale defends the boundary in one sentence; a criterion without a rationale is a boundary you cannot defend in the paper.

Example: Digital Innovation

Hund et al. (2021): what constitutes digital innovation, and which themes run across disciplines?

Hund et al. (2021) report their criteria in running text and in a process table in Appendix A; arranged as an Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria table, they show all three criterion types. Criterion 1 is a resource criterion described as a quality threshold: it bounds the search to 54 journals and excludes conferences, which matches the Coverage reading discussed for this review in SLR I. Criterion 2 operates at the search stage and is deliberately broad. Criteria 3 to 5 are question-derived, and criterion 3 defines its central term with a published definition: “the creation of (and consequent change in) market offerings, business processes, or models that result from the use of digital technology” (Nambisan et al., 2017, p. 224). Criterion 6 is a format filter applied before screening.

The authors give a rationale for each exclusion criterion, which makes the boundaries defensible. Two points are left open. Screening was carried out by the first and second author and discussed with the other two, without a reported agreement measure. And the appendix reports 318 papers from the search, while the method section and Table 1 report 423, which shows how easily counts drift when they are not logged in one place.

Selection Process

Two-Stage Screening

Screening applies the criteria in two stages: first to title and abstract, then to the full text of every record that passes.

  1. Title and abstract: fast and permissive; when in doubt, include.
  2. Full text: apply all criteria with the complete paper; record the reason for every exclusion.

Snowballing candidates pass the same two stages with the same criteria; borderline cases go into a log with decision and reason.

An abstract carries too little information for a confident exclusion, and reading every full text at the search stage is infeasible at the volumes systematic searches return. The first stage is therefore permissive: a record wrongly excluded from its abstract never returns, while a record wrongly kept costs one full-text reading. The second stage applies all criteria to the complete paper and records the criterion behind each exclusion, since the PRISMA flow diagram reports full-text exclusions with reasons.

Snowballing candidates pass through the same two stages with the same criteria (Wohlin, 2014). Keep a log of borderline cases with the decision and its reason; it shows where a criterion needs sharpening and supports the revision log of your protocol.

Pilot and Agreement

At least two researchers apply the selection criteria, and their agreement is measured before the full screening (Kitchenham & Charters, 2007).

  • Pilot: both screeners apply the criteria independently to the same sample, for example 20 records.
  • Compare: count agreements and compute Cohen’s kappa.
  • Sharpen: revise every criterion that produced disagreement, then screen the rest.
  • Resolve: disagreements go to discussion or a third person, by a rule fixed in advance.

Kitchenham & Charters (2007) recommend that at least two researchers apply the selection criteria and that their agreement be measured. The protocol template asks for a pilot: both screeners apply the criteria independently to the same small sample, compare their decisions, and sharpen every criterion that produced disagreement. Disagreement in the pilot shows which criterion is ambiguous before it distorts the full screening.

After the pilot, teams either screen everything twice or let one person screen and the other check a sample. Either way, the protocol states how disagreements are resolved, by discussion or by a third person, and the rule is fixed before screening begins. Splitting the remaining records between screeners without a pilot leaves ambiguous criteria undetected.

Cohen’s Kappa

Cohen’s kappa measures how far two screeners agree beyond the agreement expected by chance (Cohen, 1960).

κ = (po − pe) ÷ (1 − pe)

  • po: observed agreement, the share of records both screeners decided alike.
  • pe: agreement expected by chance, given how often each screener includes.
  • Interpretation: .41 to .60 moderate, .61 to .80 substantial, above .80 almost perfect.

Raw agreement overstates reliability, because two screeners who exclude most records agree often by chance alone. Cohen’s kappa (Cohen, 1960) corrects for this: it compares the observed agreement po with the agreement pe expected if both screeners decided at random with their own inclusion rates. A kappa of 1 means perfect agreement, 0 means agreement at chance level. Landis & Koch (1977) propose the benchmarks most reviews cite: 0 to .20 slight, .21 to .40 fair, .41 to .60 moderate, .61 to .80 substantial, and .81 to 1 almost perfect agreement.

Among the five reviews, Martínez-Fernández et al. (2022) report kappa 0.751 for title and abstract screening and Heyder et al. (2023) report 0.81 for their pilot of 133 records. The other three report no agreement measure for selection.

Worked Example: Kappa

Two screeners, A and B, independently screen the same 20 records in a pilot.

  • po = (6 + 10) ÷ 20 = .80
  • pe = (9/20 × 7/20) + (11/20 × 13/20) = .158 + .358 = .515
  • κ = (.80 − .515) ÷ (1 − .515) ≈ .59: moderate

The screeners agree on 16 of 20 records, an observed agreement of 80 %. Screener A includes 9 records, screener B 7. If both decided at random with these rates, they would agree on inclusion with probability 9/20 × 7/20 ≈ .158 and on exclusion with probability 11/20 × 13/20 ≈ .358, so pe ≈ .515. Kappa is (.80 − .515) ÷ (1 − .515) ≈ .59, moderate agreement.

Eighty percent agreement sounds high; the kappa shows that a good part of it is chance. The four disagreements all point to the same question in this example: three of them are records that A included and B excluded. Reading those four records together usually reveals the ambiguous criterion.

Quality Appraisal

Appraisal Purpose

Quality appraisal decides how much an included study can contribute to the synthesis (Kitchenham & Charters, 2007).

  • Exclude: studies below a threshold leave the corpus.
  • Weight: stronger studies carry more weight; conclusions are checked with and without weak studies.
  • Describe: quality is reported per study, and readers see which findings rest on strong evidence.

Selection decides whether a study is relevant; appraisal decides how far it can be trusted. Kitchenham & Charters (2007) list several uses of quality assessment: refining the inclusion criteria, explaining differences between study results, weighting studies in the synthesis, and guiding the interpretation of findings and the strength of recommendations.

The protocol states which use applies. The five reviews show all options. Rajapakse et al. (2022) exclude papers that fail more than one of three binary criteria. Martínez-Fernández et al. (2022) score rigor and relevance and report the scores without excluding anyone, which suits a mapping study whose result is an inventory. King & He (2006) weight studies statistically by sample size and appraise nothing else. Heyder et al. (2023) use outlet rankings as a filter, which is a resource criterion. Garousi & Mäntylä (2016) appraise nothing, although most of their sources are grey literature.

Appraisal Instruments

The appraisal approach follows from the review type chosen in SLR I.

The appraisal approach follows from the review type chosen in SLR I:

  • Mapping and scoping reviews chart what exists, so most of them do not appraise study quality; the JBI guidance states that scoping reviews generally do not require critical appraisal (Peters et al., 2020). Classifying studies by type or by rigor and relevance adds information without excluding anyone.
  • Qualitative systematic reviews appraise each study. Dybå & Dingsøyr (2008) adapted a checklist from critical appraisal in health research to empirical software engineering, with eleven questions covering aims, context, research design, recruitment, control group, data collection, data analysis, reflexivity, findings, and value. The MMAT (Hong et al., 2018) offers two screening questions and five criteria for each study category (qualitative, quantitative randomized, quantitative non-randomized, quantitative descriptive, mixed methods), which helps when a corpus mixes designs.
  • Meta-analyses assess risk of bias with design-specific checklists, weight studies statistically, and test whether conclusions change when weak studies are removed.
  • Theoretical reviews judge whether a study’s argument is coherent, whether its evidence fits its claims, and whether it follows its own stated method (Boell & Cecez-Kecmanovic, 2014).
  • Multivocal reviews appraise grey sources by the authority of the producer, methodology, objectivity, date, position relative to other sources, novelty, impact, and outlet type (Garousi et al., 2019).

Checklists built for experiments do not transfer to conceptual or qualitative work: randomization and blinding have no meaning for an interview study, and scoring them produces a number without evaluative content.

Worked Example: Appraisal

A qualitative systematic review asks: how does the transparency of AI systems affect employees’ trust in them? Its corpus mixes study designs; two included studies are appraised.

Quantitative

Survey study (N = 412), moderated-mediation model of AI transparency and employee trust across three industries.

  • Sample size adequate for the model?
  • Scales previously validated?
  • Statistical assumptions checked?
  • Response bias addressed?

Qualitative

Interview study (24 informants, four organizations), process model of trust repair after an AI failure.

  • Sampling purposive and justified?
  • Coding documented, audit trail?
  • Claims triangulated across informants?
  • Researcher’s role reflected on?

Use: both studies stay in the corpus; the appraisal qualifies how much weight their findings carry in the synthesis.

The review and both papers are invented. The review is a qualitative systematic review, so it appraises each included study, and its question about AI transparency and employee trust draws on survey and interview research alike. The two papers illustrate how the appraisal questions change with the kind of evidence a paper offers. In a meta-analysis on the same question, only the survey could enter the statistical synthesis, and its appraisal would focus on risk of bias and the effect sizes it reports.

For the quantitative paper, appraisal centers on statistical adequacy: whether the sample is large enough to detect the effects the model claims, whether the measurement instruments are established, whether the assumptions behind the statistical tests hold, and whether respondents may differ systematically from non-respondents.

For the qualitative paper, appraisal centers on interpretive rigor: whether informants were selected for a defensible theoretical reason, whether a reader can trace how interview text became the reported categories, whether claims rest on several informants or sources, and whether the authors reflect on their own position. Each list fits the kind of evidence its paper offers; the MMAT (Hong et al., 2018) combines both kinds of question in one instrument for mixed corpora.

Reporting

PRISMA Flow Diagram

The PRISMA flow diagram tracks every record from identification to inclusion (Page et al., 2021).

The PRISMA 2020 statement (Page et al., 2021) includes a flow diagram that tracks every record from identification to inclusion: records identified per source, duplicates removed, records screened and excluded, reports assessed at full text and excluded with reasons, and studies included. Records found through snowballing or other methods appear in a parallel column. The full PRISMA checklist was built for health research, but the flow diagram works for every review type, and your paper template expects one.

The diagram needs the counts you log during search and screening: the search log from SLR II, the exclusions per stage, and the reasons for full-text exclusions. Counts that were not logged cannot be reconstructed afterwards, as Review E shows.

Transparency Standard

Templier & Paré (2018) found large differences in how completely reviews in top IS journals report their methods.

Your review reports three steps in enough detail for another team to reconstruct them:

  • Search: sources, strings, dates.
  • Selection: criteria, stages, counts, screeners, agreement.
  • Appraisal: instrument and use.

Templier & Paré (2018) assessed the reporting of reviews in top IS journals and found that many do not report enough to reconstruct their search and selection, regardless of the prestige of the outlet. The five reviews in this course show the same range, from C, which reports counts per criterion, to D, which reports neither its screeners nor the reasons for 52 exclusions.

Your review is held to the higher standard: another team reproduces your process in Peer-Review Workshop II. The requirement connects to replicability as introduced in Knowledge, Science and Epistemology: a selection process that cannot be reconstructed from the protocol fails the same test as an experiment that cannot be repeated.
