NoteHow to use these notes

Every sample answer draws only on facts and arguments presented in Acquired: Meta (Fall 2024, Episode 2, released 27 October 2024, approx. 6 h 17 min; transcript at acquired.fm/episodes/meta). Each answer has three parts: Episode evidence, which paraphrases the episode and names the speaker (Ben Gilbert or David Rosenthal); Concept link, which is the instructor’s mapping to course frameworks and does not come from the hosts; and a Challenge question. Searching the online transcript for the bold keyword in each evidence block takes you to the passage.

Frameworks the hosts apply themselves are labelled (hosts’ framing). These are Hamilton Helmer’s Seven Powers, “commoditize your complements”, and the town-square-to-living-room shift.

Surprising Insights

Task: Students name the most surprising or counterintuitive insight and link it to earlier classes.

Insight Episode evidence Link to previous classes
Attacking the strongest rival first David and Ben describe how Mark chose Columbia as the second school because a campus network already existed there. After winning, Facebook expanded to the schools most closely connected to Columbia to protect the position. Each school started as a separate, closed network. Entry strategy, network effects, competitive positioning
The Yahoo deal almost happened Mark initially accepted Yahoo’s USD 1 bn offer (summer 2006). He walked away only after Yahoo’s stock fell about 20% and the offer dropped to roughly USD 800 m. Less than two years later, Microsoft’s acquisition talks netted out at about USD 24 bn and ended in a USD 240 m investment at a USD 15 bn valuation. Founder control, valuation under uncertainty, strategic intent
Users protested News Feed while using it more On launch day in September 2006, News Feed generated about 30,000 angry support emails, and about 10% of users joined a protest group. The data showed engagement rising sharply. The feature also reduced page views and therefore short-term ad revenue. Customer feedback vs. behavioural data; emergent strategy
Mobile flipped within about 18 months of the IPO Facebook went public in 2012 with almost no mobile monetisation, after a failed HTML5 approach. By Q4 2013, mobile was 53% of revenue, and total ad revenue grew 76%, which means desktop revenue shrank. Crisis-driven strategic change, dynamic capabilities
Instagram was bought in the IPO quiet period The company bought Instagram for USD 1 bn in April 2012, during the IPO quiet period. Instagram had 27 m users and no revenue, Facebook had 900 m users, and Facebook’s previous largest acquisition was USD 70 m. The deal was done over a weekend. Acquisition logic, real options, managerial discretion
Reality Labs needs iPhone-level success to break even Reality Labs has run about USD 60 bn in operating losses since 2019. Ben’s back-of-the-envelope estimate: even if Meta launched a product as successful as the iPhone immediately, cumulative cash flows would stay negative until at least 2035. Investment appraisal, strategic bets
The hosts dispute the Cambridge Analytica narrative Ben cites the UK regulator’s report: the data was derived from a quiz and the profiling method was of doubtful effectiveness. Both hosts stress that the damage to Facebook’s brand was nonetheless enormous. Reputation, stakeholder perception, ethics (Section 7)

Prompt if discussion stalls: “Which of these episodes would a strategic plan written in 2004 have predicted?”

Competition & Positioning

Task: Map one major competitive move to a framework from the chapter.

Sample answer A – Closed college networks vs. Friendster and MySpace (2004–2006)

  • Episode evidence: Friendster was open to everyone, and new users often found no friends there. Facebook limited access to authenticated students of one school, so each new user joined an active network. Ben explicitly applies Seven Powers: Facebook counter-positioned against open global networks by accepting lower growth and a capped user base in exchange for a closed, highly engaged community (hosts’ framing). About 70% of everyone who had ever signed up was active on a given day. Ben also points to Friendster’s technical architecture and MySpace’s lack of strong engineering talent as competitor weaknesses.
  • Concept link: Counter-positioning, focus/niche strategy, direct and indirect network effects, Porter’s differentiation.
  • Challenge question: Why could incumbents with more users not copy the closed-network model?

Sample answer B – Buy or copy: Instagram, WhatsApp, Snap (2012–2016)

  • Episode evidence: Ben reads Mark’s February 2012 email, made public in a court case, on the logic of the Instagram acquisition. The plan was to keep the acquired product running, build its social mechanics into Facebook within 12–24 months, and thereby “buy time” against new competitors. Facebook acquired WhatsApp (USD 19 bn, February 2014). The company tried repeatedly to buy Snap, failed, and copied Stories into Instagram. David adds that Instagram improved the format by ranking the row of stories algorithmically. Data from the Onavo VPN app (acquired 2013) alerted Facebook to the rise of WhatsApp and Snap.
  • Concept link: Five Forces (threat of new entrants and substitutes), imitation as a competitive response, horizontal integration, competitive intelligence.
  • Challenge question: Where does pre-emptive acquisition end and anti-competitive behaviour begin?

Sample answer C – Defeating Google+ (2011)

  • Episode evidence: Ben describes how Facebook treated Google+ as an existential threat and responded with a company-wide lockdown, launching video calls and list features to match Hangouts and Circles. A single monolithic codebase allowed engineers to be reassigned quickly. David recalls Mark’s internal reference to Carthage must be destroyed.
  • Concept link: Competitive dynamics (action–response), organisational agility as a source of competitive advantage.
  • Challenge question: Which internal capabilities made this response possible, and could Google have expected it?

Sample answer D – TikTok changes the basis of competition (late 2010s–2022)

  • Episode evidence: Ben states that TikTok blindsided Facebook because its AI shows content from anyone, so the user’s own social network stops mattering. Facebook’s remaining advantage was habit, which Ben calls thin. David argues that the shift of social interaction to private messaging separated media from social, which made TikTok possible. Facebook responded with Reels and accepted revenue cannibalisation, because Reels initially monetised worse than the feed.
  • Concept link: Disruption, shifting industry boundaries, erosion of network effects, generic strategy repositioning.
  • Challenge question: In the hosts’ Seven Powers analysis, which power still protects Meta against TikTok? (Expected: scale economies in recommender systems and GPUs, per David.)

Strategy Formation

Task: Identify one deliberate and one emergent strategy and evaluate how each played out.

Type Example from the episode How it played out
Deliberate Summer 2005 roadmap. Mark’s six-point plan: redesign, photos, News Feed, events, local business, and a platform (“I’m bored” feature). David notes that the company delivered the plan within about two years, which made it a largely realised intended strategy.
Deliberate Developer platform (F8, May 2007). Leadership believed Facebook would become a platform company. Platform drove growth but proved short-lived (David: “as short-lived as it was”). The open data access it required later fed the Cambridge Analytica crisis. This is a partly unrealised strategy with lasting side effects.
Deliberate FAIR (2013) and Oculus (2014). After the mobile crisis, Mark and Mike Schroepfer set out to avoid being caught off guard by the next platform shift again. They recruited Yann LeCun for focused AI research and bought Oculus for USD 2 bn. FAIR paid off within about two years in feed and ad ranking. Reality Labs remains a costly long-term bet (about USD 60 bn in losses).
Deliberate Open-source Llama. Ben explains it as “commoditize your complements,” the same logic as the Open Compute Project. Ongoing. It reduces dependence on closed model providers (hosts’ framing).
Emergent Advertising as the core business. Ads were first outsourced to Microsoft (2006), and Facebook’s own ad product, Beacon, failed. After Sheryl Sandberg joined, she led an open review of whether Facebook should be in the ads business at all. The intended platform strategy gave way to a realised advertising strategy.
Emergent Native mobile ads (2012–2013). The HTML5 bet failed. Boz and Will Cathcart first placed brand pages in the “people you may know” carousel as a stopgap. App-install ads grew because demand emerged from game developers. Ben: necessity is the mother of invention. Within about 18 months, mobile became the best ad unit Facebook had ever had.
Emergent Reels and the AI-recommended feed. This was a reaction to TikTok. David notes that FAIR’s 2013 investment unexpectedly became the basis for Reels (“No AI, no reels”). A deliberate strategy produced an unplanned payoff.
Emergent → formalised Town square to living room. Mark recognised the pattern from Snap and WhatsApp data and announced it as the core product strategy in 2019. Mintzberg’s pattern-in-a-stream-of-actions, later turned into a stated strategy.

Discussion notes

  • Mobile pivot: foresight or necessity? David calls the episode’s own account “slightly too charitable.” The first reaction was to hope that HTML5 would save the company. The native rewrite happened only after the app performed poorly in the market.
  • Reality Labs: deliberate or reactive? Ben offers two readings: a stand-alone business bet (break-even after 2035 at the earliest) and a hedge against platform dependence on Apple and Google, costing about 1% of market cap per year. David adds a third: owning the next developer platform. Mark has said the core apps could be twice as profitable on Meta’s own platform. All three readings are deliberate, and the trigger (ATT, platform dependence) is reactive.
  • Hosts’ summary: Ben describes product development at Meta as “an act of discovery.” The quintessence of the company is that it “moves like water” (Ben). David points to Mark’s 2005 CS50 lecture, in which Mark praised Microsoft’s approach of improving a weak first version over the third and fourth iterations.

Resources & Capabilities

Task: Apply VRIO to a resource bundle and analyse how Meta renewed it.

Sample answer A – Engagement and advertising engine

Bundle: Authenticated social network (3.3 bn daily active people across the family of apps) × feed engagement × targeting engine × a single advertiser dashboard for Facebook and Instagram.

VRIO Assessment based on the episode
Valuable Ben calls Facebook and Instagram ads together the “lion’s share” of revenue, and says the single dashboard across both apps creates real synergy.
Rare David sees Google as the only similar business model. Hosts’ Seven Powers: scale economies make Meta the default channel for advertisers.
Inimitable Network economies remain strong in social products. ATT (2021) showed that a platform owner can weaken targeting: the CFO estimated a USD 10 bn revenue hit for 2022, and the stock fell 26% in one day.
Organised Shared monetisation infrastructure across apps. Advantage+ rebuilt targeting with other data signals after ATT.

Result: A sustained advantage, exposed to the platform power of Apple and Google.

Sample answer B – AI, infrastructure and engineering bundle

Bundle: FAIR (since 2013) × ranking expertise × GPU-scale infrastructure × engineering talent density × in-house tooling (HipHop for PHP, Hack).

VRIO Assessment based on the episode
Valuable David notes that Meta earns a guaranteed return on its AI spending through feed and ad ranking.
Rare David argues that recommender systems require a very high minimum investment, which keeps rising as ByteDance and Meta invest.
Inimitable Ben lists several tools Meta built for its own needs (HipHop, Hack, a NoSQL database). Facebook is its own and only infrastructure customer, so systems are purpose-built.
Organised AI is organised as a centre of excellence shared across all apps (see Section 6).

Sample answer C – Integrity, safety and privacy capability

  • Episode evidence: David names the integrity and safety team, along with privacy, security and anti-spam work, as a cornered resource (hosts’ framing). His example: a start-up trying to build a hate-speech classifier in Farsi. Ben adds the relationships with public-policy stakeholders in about 200 countries.
  • VRIO note: A resource that grew out of past crises (content moderation, FTC consent decree) and is now rare and costly to imitate.

Dynamic capabilities

Capability Evidence from the episode
Sensing Onavo usage data. Mark’s reading of the town-square-to-living-room shift. Mark and Mike Schroepfer looking for the next platform shift in 2013.
Seizing Instagram deal closed over a weekend. Oculus bought 34 days after WhatsApp. Stories and Reels launched as copies. ATT and TikTok tackled at the same time.
Transforming Native mobile rewrite (about one year). Ad engineers moved from desktop to mobile. Reorganisation into centres of excellence. Growth team founded in 2008 when user growth stalled.

Process power (hosts’ framing): Ben counts the growth team’s “10,000 little things” and the experimentation and deployment infrastructure (feature flags, automatic rollbacks, parallel experiments) as process power. David applies Helmer’s test: does the advantage carry over to newly hired staff?

Discussion prompts: Which resources could lose value? Ben argues the network moat is weaker against TikTok and calls habit “pretty thin.” Hosts’ brand assessment: negative brand power for Meta and Facebook, positive for Instagram.

Organization & Structure

Task: Pick one structural choice and assess how it supported or hindered strategy.

Sample answer A – Founder control

  • Episode evidence: Sean Parker restructured the company so that Mark would permanently control the board. Ben calls the Accel Series A the first post-dot-com institutional VC deal in which the founder kept control. After the IPO crash (−53.5%, low point September 2012), Ben argues that a board focused on short-term shareholder value would have panicked. In 2012, Sheryl Sandberg agreed with Mark to sacrifice desktop revenue for mobile, reasoning that nobody could fire Mark and only Mark could fire her. In 2022, Ben, citing Ben Thompson, calls the decision to respond to TikTok in the middle of the ATT crisis “founder-led.”
  • Hindered strategy / limits: Ben stresses survivorship bias. David notes there is no causal link from founder control to good decisions; the model works only if the founder is right most of the time.
  • Concept link: Corporate governance, strategic leadership, upper echelons.

Sample answer B – One codebase, engineers hired into the company

  • Episode evidence: Ben describes the deliberate choice of a monolithic codebase at a time when the industry was moving to microservices. Engineers are hired into the company, not into a team, and can be reassigned quickly, as in the Google+ lockdown. The trade-off: the infrastructure team must make a very large codebase manageable.
  • Concept link: Organic structure, resource fluidity, speed versus specialisation.

Sample answer C – Centres of excellence after TikTok

  • Episode evidence: David describes Meta’s current structure, which he sees as influenced by ByteDance. FAIR, infrastructure, monetisation, growth, and integrity and safety are shared functions serving all apps. The individual apps keep their own heads, who report to Chris Cox.
  • Supported strategy: Rapid transfer of AI and ranking capabilities into every product, such as Stories ranking and Reels.
  • Concept link: Matrix/front-back organisation, centralisation versus decentralisation.

Sample answer D – Running acquired apps separately

  • Episode evidence: Mark’s 2012 email: keep Instagram running as its own product and build its mechanics into Facebook. Ben notes that WhatsApp never became a competitor and serves its own use case. Integration took place mainly in advertising (one dashboard).
  • Concept link: Structural ambidexterity; exploration in separate units, exploitation through shared infrastructure.

Sample answer E – Growth team inside product (2008)

  • Episode evidence: After Beacon failed and user growth stalled, Chamath Palihapitiya, Alex Schultz, Naomi Gleit and Javier Olivan formed what Ben calls probably the first growth team. Marketing and product were combined in one team close to product, with data as the guiding principle. One example is translation crowdsourced from users.
  • Concept link: Structure following strategy (Chandler), cross-functional integration.

Further discussion material: Ben notes that Meta is its own and only infrastructure customer, so internal teams need very close coordination; he contrasts this with Amazon’s interface-based decoupling. The hosts also highlight the long tenure of the executive team.

Ethics & Values

Task: Choose one dilemma, propose a course of action, relate it to CSR, and evaluate trade-offs.

Sample answer A – Platform data access and Cambridge Analytica

  • Episode evidence: The early developer platform gave apps full profile data and, at first, friend lists. Terms of service required deletion but could not be enforced. Ben cites the UK report: 320,000 quiz takers exposed public profile data of 87 m users. Facebook had asked Cambridge Analytica to delete the data in 2015. Consequences: Facebook had already signed an FTC consent decree in 2011. The FTC settlement in 2019 was USD 5 bn with 20 years of monitoring. In July 2018, the stock fell 19% (USD 119 bn) in one day. Ben’s view is that Cambridge Analytica’s actual impact was “wildly oversold,” but Facebook had earned users’ distrust through its earlier behaviour.
  • Course of action: Restrictive data access by default, enforceable deletion obligations, and proactive audits of third-party apps.
  • Ethical reasoning: Ben contrasts two ways of running a company: winning first and assessing the consequences afterwards, or proceeding carefully and asking permission. This maps onto consequentialist versus duty-based (deontological) reasoning.
  • CSR link: Stakeholder theory (users, regulators), Carroll’s pyramid (legal and ethical responsibilities).
  • Trade-offs: Ben argues that Facebook would probably not have reached global scale with the cautious approach. In return, it would have avoided what he calls “time bombs.”

Sample answer B – News Feed and user expectations (2006)

  • Episode evidence: The information shown was technically no more public than before, but it was now pushed to friends rather than pulled. Mark added privacy controls (“Calm down. Breathe. We hear you.”). David sees a lasting lesson the company drew: users would get used to changes. He notes that this held for News Feed but not for later changes.
  • Ethical reasoning: Informed consent and reasonable user expectations, compared with aggregate utility (higher engagement).
  • Challenge question: Does a change that users accept in hindsight justify skipping consent?

Sample answer C – Beacon and Onavo

  • Episode evidence: Beacon pushed friends’ e-commerce purchases into the News Feed; activity dropped to zero and the product was shut down. Onavo users installed a compression or VPN tool without knowing their app usage data would inform Facebook’s competitive strategy. Ben calls this an example of Facebook putting its own interests above transparency with users.
  • CSR link: Transparency, purpose limitation for data, power asymmetry towards users.

Sample answer D – Privacy as a competitive weapon (Apple ATT)

  • Episode evidence: Ben describes privacy at Apple as both a conviction and, citing Ben Thompson, a “strategy credit.” ATT hit Facebook’s targeting, and a retailer the hosts know saw its ad costs double in 2022.
  • Discussion: Can CSR positioning by one firm be used as a strategic weapon against another? Who bears the costs (SMB advertisers)?

Sample answer E – Content moderation and teen mental health

  • Episode evidence: Ben describes Facebook as caught flat-footed by problematic content. It responded by hiring tens of thousands of contractors and building review and escalation processes. In the playbook segment, the hosts name teen mental health as Meta’s next major societal battle and potentially the largest challenge it has faced.
  • Course of action (instructor): Stakeholder mapping (teenagers, parents, regulators, advertisers); weigh engagement-based business goals against the duty of care.

Discussion prompt: Did ethical lapses undermine competitive or resource advantages? Episode-based answer: The financial impact came through regulation (FTC), stock collapses, and, indirectly, ATT. Ben’s Seven Powers view: brand is a weakness, yet other powers carry a company worth USD 1.5 trn.

Closing Reflection

Task: Identify one concept that the Meta case challenged or enriched.

Concept Challenged or enriched Takeaway based on the episode
First-mover advantage Challenged Facebook entered after Friendster and MySpace, and Instagram Stories came after Snap. Ben refers to a deposition in the Winklevoss case in which Mark says Facebook itself was not a new idea.
Network effects Enriched The hosts treat them as decisive in social products but weak against TikTok-style media.
Deliberate vs. emergent strategy Enriched Most successes emerged (ads, mobile ads, Reels). Ben’s “moves like water” and David’s “multiple bets on multiple chessboards.”
VRIO / Seven Powers Enriched Advantage rests on combined powers (scale economies, network economies, process power, cornered resource) despite brand weakness (hosts’ framing).
Founder control / governance Enriched and challenged It enabled the major turnarounds (Yahoo, IPO, ATT/TikTok). The hosts warn of survivorship bias.
Platform dependence Enriched Ben: Meta’s position in the technology stack is “not quite privileged enough,” which explains its repeated push to own a platform (developer platform, Reality Labs, Llama).
CSR and performance Challenged Trust losses were costly (FTC fine, stock drops) but did not stop long-term growth. Ben expects ongoing societal battles.

Wrap-up statement for the instructor: In the hosts’ account, Meta wins through rapid adaptation in which buying, copying and long-term bets coexist, carried by a founder-controlled organisation. Its position stays fragile because it depends on others’ platforms and on public trust.

Appendix: Coverage Check

Covered in the episode (used above): Origins and campus rollout, Sean Parker and founder control, Accel, the 2005 roadmap, the Yahoo and Viacom offers, Microsoft partnerships, News Feed, F8, Beacon, Sheryl Sandberg, the growth team, the 2011 FTC consent decree, Google+, the IPO and the mobile shift, Instagram, Onavo, Snap/Stories, WhatsApp, TikTok/Reels, FAIR, Cambridge Analytica, ATT, Oculus/Reality Labs/Orion, Llama, the Seven Powers analysis, the playbook and the quintessence.

Not covered in the episode (do not attribute to the hosts if students raise them): “Year of Efficiency” and the 2022–2023 layoffs; the 2021 rename to Meta and the term “metaverse”; Frances Haugen’s disclosures; the reported Snap purchase price; the departure of the Instagram and WhatsApp founders; dual-class shares as a term (the episode speaks of majority voting control); the FTC antitrust case; anything after October 2024.
